Site reliability engineering: SLOs, burn rates, capacity planning, load testing and incident response

Designing a system is half the job; keeping it healthy under change is the other half. This part gives the practices and the maths that let teams promise a level of reliability and know when they are breaking it.

Advanced~4 hours

Reliability as a feature, with numbers

100% reliability is the wrong target: it is impossible, and each extra nine costs far more than the one before. Instead, agree what users need, measure it, and spend the remaining slack on shipping faster.

TermMeaningExample
SLI (indicator)A measured ratio of good events to valid eventsFraction of requests that return non-5xx within 300 ms
SLO (objective)The internal target for an SLI over a window99.9% over 30 days
SLA (agreement)A contract with penalties, usually looser than the SLO99.5% or you refund credits
Error budget1 - SLO: how much failure is allowed0.1% of requests, about 43 minutes per 30 days
 
def budget_minutes(slo, days=30): return (1 - slo) * days * 24 * 60
for slo in (0.99, 0.995, 0.999, 0.9999, 0.99999):
    print(f"SLO {slo * 100:g}%: error budget {budget_minutes(slo):>8.1f} minutes per 30 days")

Output

SLO 99%: error budget    432.0 minutes per 30 days
SLO 99.5%: error budget    216.0 minutes per 30 days
SLO 99.9%: error budget     43.2 minutes per 30 days
SLO 99.99%: error budget      4.3 minutes per 30 days
SLO 99.999%: error budget      0.4 minutes per 30 days

Good SLIs describe what users experience: availability (success rate), latency (percentile under a threshold), freshness (data age), correctness, throughput. Measure at the edge or from the client side where possible, not just inside a service. Pick few SLOs per user journey.

The error budget policy

  • Budget remaining: ship features, take risks, run experiments.
  • Budget exhausted: freeze risky releases, prioritise reliability work until the window recovers.

This turns the argument between "move fast" and "stay stable" into a shared number.

How this gets asked

How many minutes of error budget does a 99.9% SLO leave over 30 days (to the nearest minute)?

0.001 x 30 x 24 x 60 = 43.2 minutes.

What is the relation between an SLO and an SLA?

  • They are identical No.
  • The SLO is the internal target, usually stricter than the contractual SLA Correct.
  • The SLA is internal No.
  • SLOs have penalties SLAs do.

The gap gives you time to react before a breach.

Self-check

Give three good SLIs for a web API.

Success rate, fraction of requests under a latency threshold, and data freshness (for pipelines).

Burn-rate alerting

A naive alert such as "error rate above 0.1%" is noisy and slow. Alert on how fast the budget is being consumed.

Burn rate = (observed error rate) / (allowed error rate). A burn rate of 1 uses exactly the whole budget by the end of the window; 14.4 exhausts a 30-day budget in about 2 days.

def burn_rate(error_rate, slo): return error_rate / (1 - slo)
def hours_to_exhaust(rate, days=30): return days * 24 / rate
slo = 0.999
for err in (0.0005, 0.001, 0.0144, 0.05):
    r = burn_rate(err, slo)
    print(f"error rate {err:.2%}: burn rate {r:>6.1f} -> budget exhausted in {hours_to_exhaust(r) / 24:>7.2f} days")

Output

error rate 0.05%: burn rate    0.5 -> budget exhausted in   60.00 days
error rate 0.10%: burn rate    1.0 -> budget exhausted in   30.00 days
error rate 1.44%: burn rate   14.4 -> budget exhausted in    2.08 days
error rate 5.00%: burn rate   50.0 -> budget exhausted in    0.60 days

The multi-window pattern pages a human only when both a long and a short window show a high burn, which avoids flapping and detects fast fires quickly.

Alert on symptoms (users hurt), keep cause metrics (CPU, queue depth) on dashboards. Every page should be urgent, actionable and need a human.

How this gets asked

A service has a 99.9% SLO and currently 1.44% of requests fail. What is the burn rate?

0.0144 / 0.001 = 14.4.

Why combine a long and a short window in burn-rate alerts?

  • To save money No.
  • The long window shows the issue is significant, the short window shows it is still happening, reducing false pages and speeding detection Correct.
  • To avoid dashboards No.
  • To sample less No.

It balances precision and recall of alerts.

Self-check

What is burn rate?

Observed error rate divided by the error rate the SLO allows; 1 means the budget lasts exactly the window.

Capacity planning and queueing

Systems slow dramatically as utilisation approaches 100%, because queues build. For a single-server queue with random arrivals (M/M/1), average time in system is 1 / (service_rate - arrival_rate), so with utilisation u = arrival / service, response time grows like 1 / (1 - u).

service_ms = 10                                    # time to process one request
for util in (0.3, 0.5, 0.7, 0.8, 0.9, 0.95, 0.99):
    response = service_ms / (1 - util)
    print(f"utilisation {util:>4.0%}: average response {response:>7.1f} ms ({response / service_ms:>5.1f}x the service time)")

Output

utilisation  50%: average response    20.0 ms (  2.0x the service time)
utilisation  70%: average response    33.3 ms (  3.3x the service time)
utilisation  80%: average response    50.0 ms (  5.0x the service time)
utilisation  90%: average response   100.0 ms ( 10.0x the service time)
utilisation  95%: average response   200.0 ms ( 20.0x the service time)

Lessons: run steady-state utilisation around 50 to 70% to leave headroom for spikes and failures; the last 20% of capacity is not usable capacity. Little's Law: L = lambda x W (average number in system = arrival rate x average time in system). If 2,000 requests/s each spend 50 ms in the system, about 100 requests are in flight, so you need at least that many concurrent workers or connections.

import math
def servers_needed(peak_qps, qps_per_server, headroom=0.6, failure_domains=3, tolerate_lost=1):
    usable = qps_per_server * headroom
    base = math.ceil(peak_qps / usable)
    survive = math.ceil(peak_qps / (usable)) + math.ceil(base / failure_domains) * tolerate_lost   # N + one zone's worth
    return base, survive
base, safe = servers_needed(peak_qps=30_000, qps_per_server=1_500)
print(f"30k QPS peak, 1.5k QPS per server at 60% target utilisation: {base} servers; {safe} to survive losing one of 3 zones")
print("Little's law: in-flight requests =", 2000 * 0.050)

Output

30k QPS peak, 1.5k QPS per server at 60% target utilisation: 34 servers; 46 to survive losing one of 3 zones
Little's law: in-flight requests = 100.0

Capacity planning steps: forecast demand (growth, launches, seasonality), measure per-unit capacity with load tests, add headroom for failures and deployments (N+1 or N+2), and track the cost.

How this gets asked

Why avoid running servers at 95% utilisation?

  • It wastes power Not the main reason.
  • Queueing delay grows sharply near saturation, so latency explodes and there is no headroom for failures Correct.
  • Hardware breaks No.
  • It violates licences No.

Response time grows like 1/(1 - utilisation).

By Little's Law, 2,000 requests/s with 50 ms average time in system means about how many requests in flight?

L = 2000 x 0.05 = 100.

Self-check

What does N+1 mean?

Enough capacity to serve peak load even after losing one unit (server, zone), plus headroom.

Testing reliability

TestQuestion it answers
Load testDoes it meet SLOs at expected peak? What is the breaking point?
Stress testHow does it fail beyond capacity? Does it recover?
Soak testDo leaks and slow degradation appear over hours?
Spike testCan it absorb a sudden 10x burst (autoscaling lag, cold caches)?

Load-test with a realistic mix and data, measure latency percentiles (watch for coordinated omission: closed-loop test clients slow down when the server does, hiding the true tail), and test the dependencies' limits too. Start chaos experiments small, in staging, with a clear abort condition, then graduate to production with a limited blast radius.

# The load generator intends to send one request every 10 ms. The server is normally instant,
# except for one 10-second stall that begins at t = 5 s.
interval, total_ms, stall_start, stall_end = 10, 20_000, 5_000, 15_000
def server_latency_at(t):
    return (stall_end - t) + 1 if stall_start <= t < stall_end else 1          # a request during the stall waits for it to end
# Closed loop: the client sends the next request only after the previous reply arrives
closed, t = [], 0
while t < total_ms:
    lat = server_latency_at(t); closed.append(lat); t += max(lat, interval)
# Open loop: requests are issued on schedule whatever the server is doing, like real arriving users
open_loop = [server_latency_at(t) for t in range(0, total_ms, interval)]
def pct(xs, p): return sorted(xs)[int(len(xs) * p / 100)]
print(f"closed loop: {len(closed):>5} samples, p50 {pct(closed, 50)} ms, p99 {pct(closed, 99)} ms")
print(f"open loop  : {len(open_loop):>5} samples, p50 {pct(open_loop, 50)} ms, p99 {pct(open_loop, 99)} ms")

Output

closed loop:  1001 samples, p50 1 ms, p99 1 ms
open loop  :  2000 samples, p50 11 ms, p99 9811 ms

The closed-loop client recorded the stall as a single slow sample and reported a p99 of about a millisecond, while the open-loop view shows that about half of the planned requests were delayed, with p99 near ten seconds. Real users keep arriving during an outage, so the open-loop number is the honest one.

How this gets asked

What is coordinated omission?

  • A caching bug No.
  • A load tester that waits for slow responses and so under-reports tail latency Correct.
  • A TLS error No.
  • A database lock No.

Use open-loop load generators to model real arrival rates.

Incident response

An incident is any event that threatens an SLO. The process:

  1. Detect (alert, customer report). Declare early; declaring costs little.
  2. Roles: incident commander (coordinates, decides, does not debug), operations/tech lead (investigates and mitigates), communications lead (updates stakeholders and status page), scribe (timeline).
  3. Mitigate first, diagnose later: roll back, fail over, disable a feature flag, shed load, scale out. Restoring service beats finding the root cause.
  4. Communicate at a steady cadence with what you know, what you do not, and the next update time.
  5. Resolve and verify with the SLI.
  6. Learn: blameless postmortem.

Severity levels (SEV1 to SEV4) set response expectations: SEV1 is customer-visible, broad impact, all hands, exec notification.

Debugging method for the on-call

Start from symptoms to scope to recent change: What changed? (deploy, flag, config, traffic, dependency, certificate, quota). Compare healthy and unhealthy hosts or regions. Follow a failing request with a trace id across services. Use the RED method for services (Rate, Errors, Duration) and USE for resources (Utilisation, Saturation, Errors). Check dependencies and shared infrastructure (DNS, database, cache, queue) before blaming your own code.

How this gets asked

During an outage, what comes first?

  • Finding the root cause Later.
  • Mitigating impact (rollback, failover, flag off), then diagnosing Correct.
  • Writing the postmortem After resolution.
  • Assigning blame Never.

Restore service, then learn why.

Self-check

Name the incident roles.

Incident commander, operations lead, communications lead and scribe.

Blameless postmortems

The aim is learning, not blame: people act reasonably given what they knew, so look for systemic causes (missing guardrails, unclear ownership, poor alerts). A good postmortem has: summary and impact (users, duration, SLO burn), timeline with timestamps, contributing factors (not a single root cause), what went well, what went badly, where we got lucky, and action items with owners and dates, prioritised and tracked.

Use "five whys" carefully: ask until you reach a fixable process or design gap, not a person. Follow up: unfinished action items are the most common reason incidents repeat.

Self-check

What makes a postmortem blameless?

It examines systemic causes and contributing factors rather than individuals, so people report honestly and fixes address the system.

Toil, automation and runbooks

Toil is manual, repetitive, automatable work that scales with the service and has no lasting value (restarting a process, hand-running a migration). SRE teams cap toil (often 50% of time) and automate the rest. Write runbooks: for each alert, what it means, how to confirm, the first mitigation steps, who to escalate to. Link the runbook from the alert. Prefer self-healing (automatic restarts, autoscaling, traffic shifting) but keep humans in the loop for risky actions.

Operational readiness checklist

  • SLIs and SLOs defined; dashboards and alerts follow them; burn-rate alerts configured.
  • Health checks, timeouts, retries with backoff, circuit breakers, load shedding, graceful degradation.
  • Capacity headroom for N+1; autoscaling tested; quotas and limits known (also in dependencies).
  • Backups verified by restoring; disaster recovery rehearsed.
  • Deployments gradual with automatic rollback; feature flags for kill switches.
  • On-call rotation, escalation paths, runbooks, status page and communication templates.
  • Logs, metrics and traces with correlation ids; retention and cost controlled.
  • Security: patch process, secret rotation, access reviews, audit logs.
Key idea

Define reliability with SLOs and an error budget, alert on burn rate, keep utilisation well below saturation, test failures on purpose, mitigate before diagnosing, and learn from incidents with blameless postmortems whose action items actually get done.

Interview questions: SRE and operations

14 questions. Try to answer aloud in under a minute, then reveal. Tags show the level. All questions are also in the interview bank.

Basic SLI, SLO and SLA?

SLI is the measured indicator, SLO the internal target for it, SLA the contractual promise with penalties.

Basic What is an error budget?

The allowed unreliability, 1 minus the SLO. If budget remains you can ship risky changes; when exhausted you prioritise reliability work.

Medium How many minutes of downtime does 99.9% allow per 30 days?

About 43 minutes (0.1% of 43,200 minutes).

Medium What is burn rate and how do you alert on it?

Error rate divided by the allowed error rate. Page when a high burn rate is seen over both a long and a short window; ticket for slow burns.

Medium Why not alert on CPU usage?

It is a cause, not a user-visible symptom; alert on symptoms such as errors and latency, and keep cause metrics for diagnosis.

Medium Why keep utilisation around 60 to 70%?

Queueing delay rises sharply near saturation and you need headroom for spikes and failures.

Medium State Little's Law with an example.

Average items in system = arrival rate x average time in system; 2,000 requests/s at 50 ms means about 100 in flight.

Medium What roles exist during an incident?

Incident commander, operations lead, communications lead and scribe; the commander coordinates rather than debugging.

Medium What belongs in a blameless postmortem?

Impact, timeline, contributing factors, what went well and badly, and owned, dated action items, without blaming individuals.

Advanced What is coordinated omission in load testing?

A closed-loop client that waits for slow responses sends fewer requests during stalls, hiding the latency real arriving users would see. Use open-loop arrival schedules.

Advanced How do you test resilience?

Load, stress, soak and spike tests, failure injection and chaos experiments with a limited blast radius, game days, and canary or shadow traffic for releases.

Advanced RED versus USE methods?

RED for services: Rate, Errors, Duration. USE for resources: Utilisation, Saturation, Errors.

Advanced What is toil and how do you handle it?

Manual, repetitive, automatable work that scales with the service. Cap it, automate it and use runbooks and self-healing.

Advanced How do you plan capacity for a launch?

Forecast demand, load test per-unit capacity, add N+1 or N+2 headroom for failures and deployments, check dependency quotas and plan autoscaling lead time and cost.
