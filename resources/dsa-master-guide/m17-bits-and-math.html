Binary numbers and bitwise operators

Important Topic 17.1

Simple definition

Computers store integers in binary (bits that are 0 or 1). Bitwise operators work on those bits directly: AND (&), OR (|), XOR (^), NOT (~), left shift (<<), right shift (>>).

Why it matters

Bit tricks give O(1) solutions to parity, power-of-two, counting bits and finding the unique number.

Real-world analogy

A row of light switches: each switch (bit) is on or off; AND/OR/XOR describe how two rows of switches combine.

From scratch: why this idea exists and how you could invent it

A computer is built from switches that are on or off. Everything it stores, numbers, letters, pictures, is a pattern of those switches: bits. Binary is the number system that uses two digits, just as decimal uses ten. Position values double instead of multiplying by ten: 1101 = 8 + 4 + 0 + 1 = 13.

Because numbers are bit patterns, you can manipulate them directly. AND with a mask keeps only chosen bits. OR switches bits on. XOR flips where bits differ. A shift multiplies or divides by powers of two. These operations map to single CPU instructions, which is why they are the fastest operations a computer has.

The tricks in interviews are not magic; each follows from how binary works. n & (n - 1) clears the lowest set bit: subtracting 1 flips the lowest set bit and all zeros below it, so AND-ing wipes exactly that bit. XOR of a value with itself is 0, so pairs cancel; x ^ 0 = x. These facts are enough to derive most solutions.

Bitmasks represent sets compactly: bit i on means item i is present. Iterating mask from 0 to 2^n - 1 visits all subsets, and operations on sets (union, intersection, membership) become OR, AND, and shift-and-test.

Visual explanation (interactive)

Step-by-step walkthrough

  1. Binary 13 = 1101 = 8 + 4 + 0 + 1. Python: bin(13), int("1101", 2).
  2. a & b: 1 only where both are 1. a | b: 1 where either is 1. a ^ b: 1 where bits differ. x ^ x = 0, x ^ 0 = x.
  3. x << k multiplies by 2^k; x >> k divides by 2^k (floor).
  4. Odd/even: x & 1 is 1 for odd. Check bit i: (x >> i) & 1. Set bit i: x | (1 << i). Clear bit i: x & ~(1 << i). Toggle: x ^ (1 << i).
  5. Power of two: n > 0 and n & (n - 1) == 0. Count set bits: repeat n &= n - 1.
  6. Find the unique element when all others appear twice: XOR everything.
  7. Use the calculator to click bits on and off and see all operators update.

Deep dive in simple words

Every integer is stored as bits (0 or 1). 13 is 1101 in binary: 8 + 4 + 0 + 1. Bitwise operators work on every bit at the same time. AND (&) gives 1 only if both bits are 1; OR (|) gives 1 if either is 1; XOR (^) gives 1 if the bits are different; NOT (~) flips bits.

Shifts: x << k moves bits left, multiplying by 2^k. x >> k moves bits right, dividing by 2^k and rounding down. 1 << i makes a number with only bit i on. This is how you build masks.

The four key bit tricks: check bit i with (x >> i) & 1; set it with x | (1 << i); clear it with x & ~(1 << i); toggle it with x ^ (1 << i). x & 1 is 1 for odd numbers.

Two more favourites: x & (x - 1) removes the lowest set bit (this gives power-of-two checks and counting set bits). XOR properties (x ^ x = 0, x ^ 0 = x, order does not matter) find the single non-repeating number.

Bitmasks represent subsets: with n items, each number from 0 to 2^n - 1 is a subset (bit i on means item i is chosen). This enumerates all subsets without recursion.

Under the hood: what really happens inside Python

  • Python ints are arbitrary precision and signed with two's complement semantics: ~5 is -6, and -1 has infinitely many leading ones. To work in 32 bits mask with & 0xFFFFFFFF.
  • bin(x) shows the binary string; x.bit_count() (3.10+) counts set bits; int(s, 2) parses binary.
  • x & -x isolates the lowest set bit because -x is ~x + 1, which keeps that bit and flips everything above it.

Why? and Why not?

Why do bit tricks exist?

Computers store numbers as bits, and bit operations are the fastest and use no extra memory.

Why does n & (n - 1) clear the lowest set bit?

Subtracting 1 flips that bit and all lower zeros; AND-ing keeps only the unchanged high bits.

Why is x ^ x equal to 0?

XOR gives 1 only where bits differ, and a number never differs from itself.

Why use parentheses with bit operators in Python?

== and comparisons bind tighter than &, so x & 1 == 1 is parsed as x & (1 == 1).

Why does ~5 equal -6 in Python?

~x = -x - 1 in two's complement with unlimited sign bits.

GCD, LCM, primes and divisibility

Important Topic 17.2

Simple definition

The GCD (greatest common divisor) is the largest number dividing both inputs; the LCM (least common multiple) is the smallest number both divide. A prime has exactly two divisors: 1 and itself.

Why it matters

These show up in "simplify a fraction", "when do two events coincide", prime counting and divisor problems.

Real-world analogy

Two traffic lights blinking every 12 and 18 seconds: the LCM (36) tells you when they flash together; the GCD (6) is the largest step that divides both timings.

From scratch: why this idea exists and how you could invent it

Number theory starts from divisibility: a divides b if b is a whole number multiple of a. From this simple idea come gcd, lcm, primes and many puzzle-like questions: when do two cycles align? Can this fraction be simplified? How many divisors does n have?

GCD: the largest number dividing both. The brute force tries all candidates. Euclid (about 300 BC) noticed that gcd(a, b) = gcd(b, a mod b), because any common divisor of a and b also divides the remainder. The numbers shrink fast (at least halving every two steps), so it takes about log steps.

Primes are the building blocks: every integer factorises uniquely into primes. To test whether n is prime, you only need to check divisors up to sqrt(n): if n = a x b then one of them is at most sqrt(n). To find all primes up to N at once, the sieve avoids repeated division by crossing out multiples, starting at p squared (smaller multiples were crossed out by smaller primes).

Divisors come in pairs (d, n/d), so counting them needs only a loop to sqrt(n). Perfect squares have an odd number of divisors since one pair collapses.

These tools show up as sub-steps of bigger problems: simplifying ratios, modular inverses, cycle lengths, counting arrangements and cryptography (RSA relies on the difficulty of factoring large numbers).

Step-by-step walkthrough

  1. Euclid: gcd(a, b) = gcd(b, a % b); gcd(a, 0) = a. LCM = a // gcd * b.
  2. Python has math.gcd and math.lcm (3.9+).
  3. Primality: trial division up to sqrt(n). Sieve of Eratosthenes for all primes below N: start with all True, for each prime p cross off p*p, p*p+p, ... Complexity O(N log log N).
  4. Divisors of n come in pairs (d, n/d); loop d up to sqrt(n) to list all divisors in O(sqrt n).
  5. Prime factorisation: divide out each factor from 2 while d*d <= n; any remainder > 1 is prime.
  6. Divisibility: n % d == 0. Digit-sum divisibility by 3 and 9; last digits for 2, 5, 10.

Deep dive in simple words

GCD (greatest common divisor) is the biggest number that divides both. Euclid's trick: gcd(a, b) = gcd(b, a % b) until b is 0. It is incredibly fast (about log steps). LCM (least common multiple) = a // gcd(a, b) * b. GCD of a list: fold with gcd.

Prime numbers have exactly two divisors. To test one number, try divisors up to its square root (if it has a factor bigger than its root, it has a matching smaller one). To find all primes up to N use the Sieve of Eratosthenes: mark multiples of each prime as not prime; start at p * p.

Divisors come in pairs (d, n / d) so counting or listing them needs a loop only up to sqrt(n). Perfect squares have an odd number of divisors because one pair is the same number twice.

Prime factorisation: divide n by 2, 3, 4... while d * d <= n, collecting each factor; if something > 1 is left, it is a prime factor. This powers questions about number of divisors, smallest prime factor and so on.

Divisibility quick facts: sum of digits divisible by 3 or 9, last digit for 2, 5 and 10, last two digits for 4. In code simply use n % d == 0.

Under the hood: what really happens inside Python

  • Euclid's algorithm is iterative: a, b = b, a % b. Python provides math.gcd (and math.lcm from 3.9).
  • The sieve needs O(N) memory of booleans; a bytearray makes it compact and fast. Time O(N log log N).
  • math.isqrt(n) gives an exact integer square root without floating point errors, unlike int(n ** 0.5) for very large n.

Python implementation

def gcd(a, b):
    while b:
        a, b = b, a % b
    return a

def lcm(a, b):
    return a // gcd(a, b) * b

def sieve(n):                             # primes <= n
    is_p = [True] * (n + 1)
    is_p[0:2] = [False, False]
    for p in range(2, int(n ** 0.5) + 1):
        if is_p[p]:
            for m in range(p * p, n + 1, p):
                is_p[m] = False
    return [i for i, ok in enumerate(is_p) if ok]

def prime_factors(n):
    res, d = [], 2
    while d * d <= n:
        while n % d == 0:
            res.append(d)
            n //= d
        d += 1
    if n > 1:
        res.append(n)
    return res

Stage 1 - test every divisor below n

O(n) per number.

def count_primes(n):
    def is_p(x):
        return x > 1 and all(x % d for d in range(2, x))
    return sum(is_p(x) for x in range(n))

Check: count_primes(30) returns 10

Stage 2 - only test up to the square root

A composite number has a factor at most sqrt(x): O(sqrt x) per number.

def count_primes(n):
    def is_p(x):
        if x < 2:
            return False
        d = 2
        while d * d <= x:
            if x % d == 0:
                return False
            d += 1
        return True
    return sum(is_p(x) for x in range(n))

Check: count_primes(30) returns 10

Stage 3 - the sieve

Cross out multiples of each prime starting at p*p: O(n log log n) for ALL numbers at once.

def count_primes(n):
    if n < 3:
        return 0
    sieve = [True] * n
    sieve[0] = sieve[1] = False
    for p in range(2, int(n ** 0.5) + 1):
        if sieve[p]:
            for m in range(p * p, n, p):
                sieve[m] = False
    return sum(sieve)

Check: count_primes(30) returns 10

Why? and Why not?

Why stop trial division at sqrt(n)?

If n = a x b, one of a or b is at most sqrt(n).

Why does Euclid's algorithm work?

Any common divisor of a and b also divides a mod b, so gcd(a, b) = gcd(b, a mod b).

Why start crossing out at p*p in the sieve?

Smaller multiples have a smaller prime factor and are already crossed out.

Why compute lcm as a // gcd * b?

Dividing first avoids a huge intermediate product in languages with fixed-size integers.

Why does a perfect square have an odd number of divisors?

One divisor pair is the same number twice.

Factorials, combinations and modular arithmetic

Important Topic 17.3

Simple definition

Modular arithmetic keeps numbers small by working with remainders. Combinatorics counts arrangements: permutations (order matters) and combinations (order does not).

Why it matters

Answers in many problems are huge, so the statement says "print the answer modulo 10^9 + 7".

Real-world analogy

A clock: 10 o'clock + 5 hours is 3 o'clock because we work modulo 12.

From scratch: why this idea exists and how you could invent it

Counting questions ask 'in how many ways?'. If order matters (arranging letters), multiply choices: 5 people into a row is 5 x 4 x 3 x 2 x 1 = 5!. If order does not matter (picking a team), divide out the orderings: C(n, r) = n! / (r! (n - r)!). The pattern 'arrange then divide by the overcount' derives every formula here.

These numbers explode: 52! is about 8 x 10^67. Problem setters therefore ask for the answer modulo a prime, so that the output fits in a small number. Modular arithmetic works like a clock: you keep only the remainder after division by m.

Addition, subtraction and multiplication respect the modulus: you may reduce at every step. This is vital to keep intermediate numbers small (and fast) while still getting the right final remainder.

Division is the exception. You cannot divide remainders. Instead you multiply by the modular inverse of the divisor, which exists when the divisor and modulus are coprime. For a prime modulus p, Fermat's little theorem gives inverse(x) = x^(p-2) mod p, computed fast with pow(x, p - 2, p).

That chain of reasoning, count, then reduce, then handle division, is exactly how nCr mod p is computed in contests.

Step-by-step walkthrough

  1. Permutations of n distinct items: n!. Ordered selections of r from n: P(n, r) = n!/(n-r)!.
  2. Combinations: C(n, r) = n! / (r! (n-r)!). Pascal: C(n, r) = C(n-1, r-1) + C(n-1, r).
  3. Modular rules: (a + b) % m = ((a % m) + (b % m)) % m, same for multiplication and subtraction. Division is NOT allowed directly.
  4. Division by x modulo a prime p is multiplication by the inverse pow(x, p-2, p) (Fermat). Python: pow(base, exp, mod) is fast modular exponentiation.
  5. Python big integers never overflow, but results mod 10^9 + 7 are still required by the judge, and reducing early keeps numbers small and fast.
  6. math.factorial, math.comb(n, r), math.perm(n, r) exist in Python 3.8+.

Deep dive in simple words

Counting arrangements: a permutation is an ordered arrangement (n! ways to arrange n different items). A combination chooses items where order does not matter: C(n, r) = n! / (r! (n - r)!). Choosing a team of 3 from 10 is C(10, 3) = 120; arranging 3 of 10 people in a line is 10 x 9 x 8 = 720.

Answers can be gigantic, so problems ask for the result 'modulo 10^9 + 7' (a prime number). Modulo is the remainder. Rules: (a + b) % m = ((a % m) + (b % m)) % m and the same for multiplication. Reduce after EVERY step to keep numbers small.

Division is the catch: you cannot divide modulo m directly. With a prime modulus p, dividing by x equals multiplying by x^(p-2) mod p (modular inverse, from Fermat's little theorem). Python's pow(x, p - 2, p) computes it quickly.

Pascal's triangle gives combinations without division: C(n, r) = C(n-1, r-1) + C(n-1, r). Good when n is small (up to a few thousand).

Fast exponentiation (power by squaring): x^n in O(log n) multiplications: x^n = (x^(n/2))^2 times x if n is odd. Python's pow(x, n, mod) already does this.

Under the hood: what really happens inside Python

  • 10^9 + 7 is prime and fits in 30 bits, so the product of two residues fits in 60 bits, safe in 64-bit integers in other languages. Python needs no such care but the convention remains.
  • Python's pow(a, b, m) uses square-and-multiply in O(log b) steps.
  • Python's % returns a result with the sign of the modulus, so (a - b) % m is already non-negative.

Python implementation

MOD = 1_000_000_007

def mod_factorial(n):
    result = 1
    for i in range(2, n + 1):
        result = result * i % MOD       # reduce at every step
    return result

def nCr(n, r):
    if r < 0 or r > n:
        return 0
    num = den = 1
    for i in range(r):
        num = num * (n - i) % MOD
        den = den * (i + 1) % MOD
    return num * pow(den, MOD - 2, MOD) % MOD

Why? and Why not?

Why take the modulus at every step?

It keeps numbers small and fast and gives the same remainder because modular arithmetic respects + and x.

Why can't you divide under a modulus?

Remainders do not follow normal division; you multiply by the modular inverse instead.

Why does Fermat's little theorem give the inverse for a prime modulus?

x^(p-1) = 1 (mod p), so x^(p-2) is the inverse of x.

Why 10^9 + 7?

It is prime, fits in 30 bits and is conventional, so problems agree on one modulus.

Why not compute n! then divide?

n! is astronomically large; reduce at each step and use inverses.

Interview questions: Bit Manipulation & Mathematics

56 questions: try to answer aloud in under a minute, then reveal. All questions from every module are also in the interview bank.

Basic Conceptual How do you check if a number is a power of two?

n > 0 and n & (n - 1) == 0. A power of two has exactly one set bit and n - 1 flips it and everything below.

Basic Conceptual How do you count set bits?

Repeat n &= n - 1 until n is 0 and count the steps (Brian Kernighan), or bin(n).count('1').

Basic Conceptual Find the number that appears once when every other appears twice.

XOR all elements. Pairs cancel to 0, leaving the single number.

Basic Conceptual How do you swap two numbers without a temp variable?

a ^= b; b ^= a; a ^= b. (In Python simply a, b = b, a.)

Basic Conceptual What does x & -x give?

The lowest set bit of x (used in Fenwick trees).

Basic Conceptual How do you generate all subsets with bits?

Loop mask from 0 to 2^n - 1 and include item i when (mask >> i) & 1.

Basic Conceptual Why does n & (n - 1) clear the lowest set bit?

Subtracting 1 turns the lowest 1 into 0 and all lower zeros into ones; AND-ing with n keeps only the unchanged higher bits.

Basic Conceptual How does Euclid's algorithm work?

gcd(a, b) = gcd(b, a % b) until b = 0; the answer is a. It is O(log min(a, b)).

Basic Conceptual How do you compute the LCM?

lcm(a, b) = a // gcd(a, b) * b.

Basic Conceptual How do you check whether a number is prime efficiently?

Test divisors from 2 up to sqrt(n). O(sqrt n). For many numbers use a sieve.

Basic Conceptual What is the Sieve of Eratosthenes and its complexity?

It marks multiples of every prime as composite, starting at p * p. O(n log log n) time, O(n) space.

Basic Conceptual How do you count the divisors of n?

Loop d up to sqrt(n); each divisor d adds itself and n / d (once if they are equal). O(sqrt n).

Basic Conceptual How many trailing zeros does n! have?

Count factors of 5: n/5 + n/25 + n/125 + ...

Basic Conceptual Why does the sieve start crossing out at p * p?

Smaller multiples of p have a smaller prime factor and were already crossed out.

Basic Conceptual What is the difference between permutations and combinations?

Permutations count ordered arrangements (n!/(n-r)!); combinations count unordered selections (n!/(r!(n-r)!)).

Basic Conceptual Why do problems ask for the answer modulo 10^9 + 7?

The true answer is too large for fixed-size integers. A big prime modulus keeps numbers small and allows modular division.

Basic Conceptual How do you compute nCr modulo a prime?

Multiply the top terms, multiply the bottom terms, and multiply by the modular inverse of the bottom: num * pow(den, MOD - 2, MOD) % MOD.

Basic Conceptual Compute x^n quickly.

Square-and-multiply (binary exponentiation): O(log n). In Python pow(x, n) or pow(x, n, mod).

Basic Conceptual What is (a - b) % m when a < b?

In Python it is still non-negative (e.g. (3 - 5) % 7 = 5). In C/Java you would add m.

Basic Conceptual Why can't you divide modulo m directly?

Remainders do not support division the way integers do; (a / b) mod m is not (a mod m) / (b mod m). Multiply by the modular inverse instead.

Medium Coding Find the two numbers that appear once when all others appear twice.

XOR everything to get a ^ b. Take any set bit (lowest: x & -x) to split numbers into two groups by that bit; XOR each group separately to get a and b.

Medium Tricky output What is ~5 in Python and why?

-6. ~x equals -x - 1 because of two's complement with unlimited sign bits.

Medium Real-world How do Unix file permissions use bits?

Read, write, execute are bits (4, 2, 1) for owner, group and others; permission checks use AND with the required mask.

Medium Why / Why not Why do bit tricks exist?

Computers store numbers as bits, and bit operations are the fastest and use no extra memory.

Medium Why / Why not Why does n & (n - 1) clear the lowest set bit?

Subtracting 1 flips that bit and all lower zeros; AND-ing keeps only the unchanged high bits.

Medium Why / Why not Why is x ^ x equal to 0?

XOR gives 1 only where bits differ, and a number never differs from itself.

Medium Why / Why not Why use parentheses with bit operators in Python?

== and comparisons bind tighter than &, so x & 1 == 1 is parsed as x & (1 == 1).

Medium Why / Why not Why does ~5 equal -6 in Python?

~x = -x - 1 in two's complement with unlimited sign bits.

Medium Why / Why not Why not use bit tricks everywhere?

They hurt readability. Use them when they bring clear benefits (O(1) space, standard idioms).

return n & 1 == 1 (written as (n & 1) == 1).

Medium Coding Return the prime factorisation of n as a dictionary of prime to exponent.
def factorize(n):
    f, d = {}, 2
    while d * d <= n:
        while n % d == 0:
            f[d] = f.get(d, 0) + 1
            n //= d
        d += 1
    if n > 1:
        f[n] = f.get(n, 0) + 1
    return f

RSA keys are products of two huge primes; security relies on factoring being computationally infeasible.

Medium Why / Why not Why stop trial division at sqrt(n)?

If n = a x b, one of a or b is at most sqrt(n).

Medium Why / Why not Why does Euclid's algorithm work?

Any common divisor of a and b also divides a mod b, so gcd(a, b) = gcd(b, a mod b).

Smaller multiples have a smaller prime factor and are already crossed out.

Dividing first avoids a huge intermediate product in languages with fixed-size integers.

Medium Why / Why not Why does a perfect square have an odd number of divisors?

One divisor pair is the same number twice.

Medium Why / Why not Why not use floating point sqrt for primality?

Rounding errors can give the wrong bound for large n. Use math.isqrt or compare d * d <= n.

Medium Coding Return all divisors of n in sorted order.

Loop d up to sqrt(n); add d and n // d; sort the result.

Medium Coding Compute nCr mod p for many queries efficiently.

Precompute factorials and inverse factorials up to N once, then nCr = fact[n] * inv_fact[r] * inv_fact[n - r] % p in O(1).

Medium Real-world Why are results printed modulo 10^9 + 7 in contests?

It keeps outputs bounded, is easy to check, and lets problem setters use huge counts without big-number arithmetic.

Medium Why / Why not Why take the modulus at every step?

It keeps numbers small and fast and gives the same remainder because modular arithmetic respects + and x.

Medium Why / Why not Why can't you divide under a modulus?

Remainders do not follow normal division; you multiply by the modular inverse instead.

Medium Why / Why not Why does Fermat's little theorem give the inverse for a prime modulus?

x^(p-1) = 1 (mod p), so x^(p-2) is the inverse of x.

Medium Why / Why not Why 10^9 + 7?

It is prime, fits in 30 bits and is conventional, so problems agree on one modulus.

Medium Why / Why not Why not compute n! then divide?

n! is astronomically large; reduce at each step and use inverses.

Medium Why / Why not Why use pow(a, b, m) rather than a ** b % m?

pow with three arguments reduces during the computation and runs in O(log b) without creating a huge number.
