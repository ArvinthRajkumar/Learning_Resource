Recommender systems, ranking and bandits

Recommenders decide what billions of people see. They combine retrieval, ranking and exploration, and they are shaped by feedback loops. This part covers the core algorithms with runnable examples, the metrics, and the system design.

MediumAdvanced~5 hours

The recommendation problem

Given users, items and past interactions (clicks, purchases, ratings, watch time), predict what each user will like next. Signals: explicit feedback (ratings, likes), which is sparse, and implicit feedback (views, clicks, dwell time, purchases), which is abundant but noisy and biased (absence of a click does not mean dislike).

Collaborative filtering

Neighbourhood methods

User-based: find users similar to you, recommend what they liked. Item-based: for items you liked, recommend similar items (similarity from co-ratings). Item-based is more stable and precomputable at scale.

import math
ratings = {"ann": {"A": 5, "B": 4, "C": 1}, "bob": {"A": 4, "B": 5, "D": 2},
           "cy": {"A": 1, "C": 5, "D": 4}, "dee": {"B": 5, "C": 2, "D": 1}}
def cosine(u, v):
    common = set(u) & set(v)
    if not common: return 0.0
    num = sum(u[i] * v[i] for i in common)
    return num / (math.sqrt(sum(x * x for x in u.values())) * math.sqrt(sum(x * x for x in v.values())))
target = "ann"
sims = {u: cosine(ratings[target], ratings[u]) for u in ratings if u != target}
print({u: round(s, 2) for u, s in sims.items()})
unseen = {"D"}
for item in unseen:
    num = sum(sims[u] * ratings[u][item] for u in sims if item in ratings[u])
    den = sum(abs(sims[u]) for u in sims if item in ratings[u])
    print("predicted rating of", item, "for", target, "=", round(num / den, 2))

Output

{'bob': 0.92, 'cy': 0.24, 'dee': 0.62}
predicted rating of D for ann = 1.92

Matrix factorisation

Represent each user u and item i by a vector of k latent factors (k about 16 to 256), and predict the rating as their dot product: r_hat = p_u . q_i (plus biases). Learn the vectors by minimising squared error on observed ratings with L2 regularisation, using SGD or alternating least squares (ALS). Latent factors often correspond to interpretable tastes (action versus romance).

import random
random.seed(0)
# observed (user, item, rating) triples from a small matrix with two tastes
data = [(0, 0, 5), (0, 1, 4), (0, 2, 1), (1, 0, 4), (1, 2, 1), (1, 3, 2), (2, 1, 1), (2, 2, 5), (2, 3, 4), (3, 0, 1), (3, 2, 4), (3, 3, 5)]
k, n_users, n_items = 2, 4, 4
P = [[random.gauss(0, 0.3) for _ in range(k)] for _ in range(n_users)]
Q = [[random.gauss(0, 0.3) for _ in range(k)] for _ in range(n_items)]
lr, reg = 0.02, 0.02
for epoch in range(3000):
    for u, i, r in data:
        pred = sum(P[u][f] * Q[i][f] for f in range(k))
        e = r - pred
        for f in range(k):
            pu, qi = P[u][f], Q[i][f]
            P[u][f] += lr * (e * qi - reg * pu)
            Q[i][f] += lr * (e * pu - reg * qi)
rmse = (sum((r - sum(P[u][f] * Q[i][f] for f in range(k))) ** 2 for u, i, r in data) / len(data)) ** 0.5
print("training RMSE %.3f" % rmse)
print("predicted rating user 1 for item 1 (unobserved): %.2f" % sum(P[1][f] * Q[1][f] for f in range(k)))

Output

training RMSE 0.104
predicted rating user 1 for item 1 (unobserved): 3.15

For implicit data use losses on interactions, not ratings: BPR (rank an observed item above a random unobserved one), weighted ALS with confidence, or sampled softmax.

Cold start

New users or items have no interactions. Remedies: popularity or trending lists, onboarding questions, content features (item metadata, text and image embeddings) feeding a hybrid model, contextual signals (location, device, time), and exploration to gather data quickly.

How this gets asked

What does matrix factorisation learn?

  • A decision tree No.
  • Latent vectors for users and items whose dot product predicts ratings Correct.
  • A tokenizer No.
  • Only popularity No.

The factors capture hidden tastes and item attributes.

Which approach helps with the item cold-start problem?

  • Pure collaborative filtering Needs interactions.
  • Content features such as text and image embeddings in a hybrid model Correct.
  • Increasing k No.
  • Removing regularisation No.

Metadata lets the model score items before any clicks.

Self-check

User-based versus item-based CF?

User-based finds similar users; item-based finds items similar to those a user liked, which is more stable and precomputable.

Retrieval and ranking at scale

The funnel

Catalogues of millions cannot be scored by a heavy model per request. Use stages:

  1. Retrieval: fast, recall-oriented, from many sources (two-tower ANN search, co-visitation, popular in region, recent items) producing hundreds to a few thousand candidates.
  2. Ranking: a richer model predicts engagement (click, watch, purchase probabilities) using many features (user, item, context, cross features).
  3. Re-ranking: enforce diversity, freshness, fairness, deduplication, ad and business constraints, then slate optimisation.

Two-tower models

A user tower and an item tower (neural networks) each map to a vector; the score is their dot product. Train with a contrastive loss using in-batch negatives (A4). Item vectors are precomputed and indexed for approximate nearest neighbour search; the user vector is computed online. Correct the sampling bias of in-batch negatives (popular items are over-sampled) with a logQ correction.

import math
users = {"u1": [1.0, 0.0], "u2": [0.0, 1.0]}
items = {"i1": [0.9, 0.1], "i2": [0.1, 0.9], "i3": [0.7, 0.7]}
def top(user_vec, k=2):
    scored = sorted(items, key=lambda i: -sum(a * b for a, b in zip(user_vec, items[i])))
    return scored[:k]
for u, v in users.items():
    print(u, "->", top(v))

Output

u1 -> ['i1', 'i3']
u2 -> ['i2', 'i3']

Ranking models

Feature crosses matter (a user's category preference times the item category). Architectures: gradient-boosted trees (strong baseline), Wide and Deep, DeepFM, DCN (deep and cross), and multi-task networks that predict several outcomes (click, like, purchase, watch time) combined into a final score. Transformers over user histories (SASRec, BERT4Rec, generative retrieval) model sequences. Optimise the objective that matches value: clicks can reward clickbait; long-term satisfaction, retention and watch time with quality filters are better, though harder to measure.

How this gets asked

  • To make code longer No.
  • A heavy ranker cannot score millions of items per request, so a fast retrieval stage narrows the candidates first Correct.
  • Ranking is not needed No.
  • Retrieval is more accurate No.

The funnel balances recall and latency.

Evaluation

Offline metrics

MetricMeaning
Precision@k, Recall@kFraction of the top k that are relevant; fraction of relevant items found
MAP, MRRAverage precision over ranks; reciprocal rank of the first relevant item
nDCG@kRewards relevant items at high ranks with graded relevance
Hit rate@kWhether any held-out item appears in the top k
AUC, log lossQuality of predicted engagement probabilities
Coverage, diversity, novelty, serendipityBeyond-accuracy qualities
CalibrationPredicted probabilities match observed rates (important for ads)
 
import math
def dcg(rels, k): return sum((2 ** r - 1) / math.log2(i + 2) for i, r in enumerate(rels[:k]))
def ndcg(rels, k):
    ideal = sorted(rels, reverse=True)
    return dcg(rels, k) / dcg(ideal, k)
print("relevant item first : %.3f" % ndcg([3, 2, 0, 0], 4))
print("relevant item last  : %.3f" % ndcg([0, 0, 2, 3], 4))
rank_of_first_hit = [1, 3, 2]
print("MRR = %.3f" % (sum(1 / r for r in rank_of_first_hit) / len(rank_of_first_hit)))

Output

relevant item first : 1.000
relevant item last  : 0.508
MRR = 0.611

Pitfalls in evaluation

  • Split by time, not randomly: predicting the past from the future leaks information.
  • Offline metrics rely on logged data shaped by the old system (selection bias): items never shown have no feedback. Use inverse propensity scoring and counterfactual evaluation, or randomised exploration traffic.
  • Offline gains often fail online, so confirm with A/B tests on business metrics and guardrails (A7).
  • Position bias: users click the top slot regardless of relevance; model it (position as a feature at training, removed at serving) or use interleaving.

  • Random splits are slower No.
  • Random splits leak future behaviour into training and overstate performance Correct.
  • Time is a feature No.
  • It balances classes No.

In production you only know the past.

Logged data is biased by the previous system and offline gains often do not transfer; confirm with an A/B test.

Exploration and bandits

Explore versus exploit

A recommender that only shows what it believes best never learns about other items and can lock into a rut. A multi-armed bandit balances exploiting the best known option and exploring uncertain ones.

StrategyHow
epsilon-greedyWith probability epsilon pick a random arm, otherwise the best so far
UCBPick the arm with the highest mean plus an uncertainty bonus (optimism under uncertainty)
Thompson samplingSample from each arm's posterior and pick the best sample
Contextual bandits (LinUCB, neural)Use features of user and item to generalise across arms
 
import random
random.seed(1)
true_ctr = [0.04, 0.05, 0.08]            # click rates of 3 items, unknown to the algorithm
def run(strategy, steps=20000):
    wins, plays, reward = [0, 0, 0], [0, 0, 0], 0
    for _ in range(steps):
        if strategy == "random":
            a = random.randrange(3)
        elif strategy == "thompson":
            a = max(range(3), key=lambda i: random.betavariate(1 + wins[i], 1 + plays[i] - wins[i]))
        else:                            # epsilon greedy
            a = random.randrange(3) if random.random() < 0.1 or 0 in plays else max(range(3), key=lambda i: wins[i] / plays[i])
        click = random.random() < true_ctr[a]
        plays[a] += 1; wins[a] += click; reward += click
    return reward, plays
for s in ("random", "epsilon", "thompson"):
    r, p = run(s)
    print("%-9s total clicks %4d, plays per item %s" % (s, r, p))

Output

random    total clicks 1183, plays per item [6715, 6619, 6666]
epsilon   total clicks 1545, plays per item [685, 666, 18649]
thompson  total clicks 1625, plays per item [264, 586, 19150]

Thompson sampling and epsilon-greedy concentrate plays on the best item (the third) and collect more clicks than random assignment, while still testing the others. The cost of exploration is called regret.

How this gets asked

What does Thompson sampling do?

  • Always picks the best mean That is greedy.
  • Samples from each arm's posterior and plays the arm with the best sample Correct.
  • Plays uniformly at random No.
  • Never explores No.

Uncertain arms have wide posteriors and are sometimes sampled high, which drives exploration.

Self-check

Explore versus exploit?

Exploiting uses the best known option; exploring tests uncertain ones to learn; bandit algorithms balance them to minimise regret.

How this gets asked

A recommender trained on its own logs keeps showing already popular items. This is a:

  • Cold start No.
  • Feedback loop with popularity bias Correct.
  • Memory leak No.
  • Tokenization bug No.

Its exposure decisions shape the data it learns from.

Users click the top slot more regardless of relevance: this is bias.

Model or correct for it, for example with position features or interleaving.

System design view

Log what was shown (impressions) as well as clicks, with the model version and position, so future models and evaluation can correct bias. See the system-design guide's feed and notification cases (news feed).

Key idea

A recommender is a funnel (retrieve, rank, re-rank) trained on its own logged behaviour. The hard parts are not the model but the biases of that data: popularity, position, selection and feedback loops, which you manage with exploration, careful evaluation and online experiments.

Self-check

What should you log in a recommender?

Impressions with position, model version and context, plus clicks and outcomes, to support training and bias correction.

Interview questions: Recommender systems

12 questions. Try to answer aloud in under a minute, then reveal. Tags show the level.

Basic Collaborative versus content-based filtering?

Collaborative filtering uses behaviour of similar users or items; content-based matches item attributes to a user profile and works for new items.

Basic What is the cold-start problem?

New users or items lack interaction data. Use popularity, content features, onboarding questions and exploration.

Medium Explain matrix factorisation.

Users and items get latent vectors whose dot product predicts the rating; learn them by minimising error on observed entries with regularisation using SGD or ALS.

Medium Why a two-stage architecture?

Retrieval narrows millions of items to hundreds quickly with high recall; a heavier ranker scores those, and re-ranking applies diversity and business rules.

Medium What is a two-tower model?

Separate user and item networks produce vectors scored by a dot product; item vectors are precomputed and searched with ANN.

Medium Which metrics evaluate recommenders?

Precision and recall at k, MAP, MRR, nDCG, hit rate, AUC and log loss, plus coverage, diversity and calibration, confirmed by online A/B tests.

Medium What is position bias and how do you handle it?

Items at the top get more clicks regardless of relevance. Model position as a feature during training and fix it at serving, or use interleaving and propensity weighting.

Advanced How do bandits help recommenders?

They balance exploration and exploitation (epsilon-greedy, UCB, Thompson sampling), learning item quality faster and reducing regret; contextual bandits generalise across users and items.

Advanced What is a feedback loop in recommendation?

The system's exposure shapes future training data, reinforcing popular items and narrow tastes; counter with exploration, debiasing, diversity and randomised traffic.

Advanced How do you evaluate offline given biased logs?

Split by time, use inverse propensity scoring or doubly robust estimators, log exploration traffic, and validate with A/B tests.

Advanced How do you optimise for long-term value rather than clicks?

Use multi-task models with satisfaction, retention and quality signals, add counter-clickbait constraints, and measure long-horizon outcomes in experiments with holdouts.

Advanced Explicit versus implicit feedback losses?

Explicit ratings use squared error on observed entries. Implicit data uses ranking losses such as BPR, weighted ALS with confidence or sampled softmax because missing entries are not negatives.
