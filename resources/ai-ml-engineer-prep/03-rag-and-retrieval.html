RAG and retrieval

You know RAG from projects. This page adds precision: diagnose failures by stage with numbers, derive BM25 term by term, and compute every ranking metric by hand. Coding problems (a) and (b) build directly on this page.

Feeds coding problems a and b~2.5 hours

Basics first: what RAG is and why it works

Intuition

An LLM only knows what was in its training data, frozen at a cutoff date, and it has never seen your private documents. Retrieval-augmented generation (RAG) is an open-book exam: before answering, look up the relevant pages and put them in the prompt. The model then reads and answers from them, and can cite them.

The mechanism

  • Why not just a long context? Cost and latency scale with prompt tokens, the lost-in-the-middle effect hurts recall, and corpora are far larger than any context window. Retrieval selects what matters.
  • Why not fine-tune the facts in? Retraining for each update is slow, there are no citations, and fact injection through fine-tuning is unreliable (part 02).
  • Common RAG eval metrics (as popularized by the RAGAS framework): context precision and recall (are the retrieved chunks relevant, and do they cover the answer?), faithfulness (are the answer's claims supported by the context?), answer relevancy (does it address the question?). Plus classic retrieval metrics (recall@k, MRR, nDCG) and answer correctness against references.
  • Variants: query rewriting, HyDE (embed a hypothetical answer), parent-document retrieval, agentic RAG (the model calls search as a tool, possibly several times), GraphRAG (retrieval over an entity graph).

How this gets asked

Why use RAG instead of stuffing every document into a long context? Select all.

  • Lower cost and latency per request Yes: fewer prompt tokens.
  • Corpora are larger than context windows Yes.
  • RAG removes the need for evals No.
  • Models use the middle of very long contexts less reliably Yes: lost in the middle.

Selective retrieval keeps prompts small, relevant and citable.

Self-check

Offline vs online steps of RAG?

Offline: parse, chunk, embed, index. Online: embed the query, retrieve (and rerank), build the prompt, generate.

What is faithfulness?

Whether every claim in the answer is supported by the retrieved context.

What is HyDE?

Generate a hypothetical answer, embed it, and retrieve with that vector instead of the raw question.

Diagnose a RAG system by stage

Intuition

A RAG answer passes through a pipeline. When the final answer is wrong, the fix depends on which stage failed. Measure each stage separately. Retrieval metrics tell you whether the right chunk was found. Answer metrics tell you whether the generator used it. If retrieval finds the right chunk but the answer is still wrong, retrieval changes will not help.

Self-check

What does high recall@k with low accuracy tell you?

Retrieval is fine. Ranking at the top or generation is the problem.

What is "lost in the middle"?

LLMs use information at the beginning and end of a long context more reliably than information in the middle.

Name a stage-level metric for retrieval and one for generation.

Retrieval: recall@k. Generation: faithfulness or answer accuracy.

Bi-encoder vs cross-encoder vs ColBERT

Intuition

A bi-encoder is like summarizing every book on an index card ahead of time and comparing your question's card to them: fast, but coarse. A cross-encoder reads your question and one candidate book together, carefully: accurate, but you can only afford to do it for a handful. ColBERT keeps a card per word, a middle ground.

  • "Use a cross-encoder for first-stage search over 10M docs." That means 10M forward passes per query. Far too slow.
  • "A reranker improves recall@20." It reorders the given candidates, so recall@20 of that set does not change. It improves precision at the top (recall@3, nDCG@5).

How this gets asked

Why are cross-encoders used only for reranking a small candidate set?

  • They are less accurate than bi-encoders They are more accurate.
  • They need a forward pass per (query, doc) pair, and doc representations cannot be precomputed Correct: the cost scales with the number of candidates per query.
  • They only work with BM25 No such requirement.
  • They output vectors that are too large They output a score, not a vector.

Joint encoding is why they are accurate, and also why they cannot be indexed ahead of time.

Self-check

What does MaxSim compute in ColBERT?

For each query token, the maximum similarity to any document token. These maxima are summed.

Does reranking the top 20 change recall@20?

No. It reorders within the set, which changes recall@k for k < 20 and nDCG.

Typical two-stage setup?

Bi-encoder (or hybrid) retrieves about 50 to 100, a cross-encoder reranks, and the top 3 to 5 go to the LLM.

Embedding models, similarity and migrations

Intuition

An embedding model defines its own coordinate system. Two models, even from the same provider and with the same 1,536 dimensions, put meanings in completely different directions. Dimension 37 in model A has nothing to do with dimension 37 in model B. Comparing a query embedded by the new model against documents embedded by the old one is like comparing GPS coordinates with street addresses.

The rule

Changing the embedding model means re-embedding the entire corpus and the queries with the new model. There are no shortcuts: neither normalization nor equal dimensions make old vectors compatible.

Migration without downtime (blue/green)

  1. Create a new index (green) next to the live one (blue).
  2. Dual-write: every new or updated document is embedded with both models and written to both indexes.
  3. Backfill: re-embed the existing corpus into green in batches (watch rate limits and cost).
  4. Evaluate green on your retrieval eval set (recall@k, nDCG) and shadow-test on real traffic.
  5. Switch reads to green (feature flag, so you can roll back), keep blue for a while, then delete it.

Cosine, dot product and normalization

Similarity
cos(a, b) = (a · b) / (|a| |b|) if |a| = |b| = 1: cos(a, b) = a · b, and |a - b|^2 = 2 - 2 cos(a, b)
a · b
dot product, sum of a_i b_i
|a|
Euclidean length
  • After L2 normalization, cosine, dot product and Euclidean distance all give the same ranking. Many vector DBs use inner product on normalized vectors for speed.
  • Unnormalized dot product lets vector length leak into the score: (3,4) and (6,8) have cosine 1 but dot product 50. Some models are trained for dot product, so use the metric the model card specifies.
  • Storage: 1M vectors x 1,536 dims x 4 bytes = 6.1 GB (3.1 GB in fp16). Matryoshka-trained models can be truncated to fewer dimensions with modest loss. That only applies if the model was trained for it.

How this gets asked

Your provider releases a new 1,536-dim embedding model (same dimension as your current one). What do you do with the existing index?

  • Nothing, the dimensions match Matching shape does not mean matching meaning. The vector spaces differ.
  • Embed only new documents with the new model This mixes two incompatible spaces in one index.
  • Re-embed the whole corpus with the new model (and embed queries with it too) Correct. Use a blue/green migration with dual writes to avoid downtime.
  • Normalize the old vectors Normalization fixes length, not orientation in a different space.

Vectors are only comparable within the model that produced them.

Self-check

When do cosine and dot product give identical rankings?

When all vectors are L2-normalized.

What is dual-write in an index migration?

Writing every new or updated document to both the old and new index during the migration, so neither falls behind.

Storage for 1M x 1,536 float32 vectors?

About 6.1 GB.

BM25 from first principles

Intuition

BM25 scores a document for a query by adding up, for each query word: how rare the word is (rare words are informative) times how often it appears in the document. Two corrections apply. Repetition has diminishing returns (the 10th "cats" adds little), and long documents get discounted (they contain more words by chance).

The formula

Okapi BM25 (with the +1 idf used by Lucene and by coding problem b)
score(D, Q) = sum over distinct t in Q of idf(t) * f(t,D) * (k1 + 1) / ( f(t,D) + k1 * (1 - b + b * |D| / avgdl) ) idf(t) = ln( 1 + (N - n(t) + 0.5) / (n(t) + 0.5) )
N
number of documents
n(t)
number of documents containing t (document frequency)
f(t,D)
count of t in D (term frequency)
|D|
length of D in tokens
avgdl
average document length over all docs
k1
saturation, typically 1.2 (0 means term frequency is ignored, large means nearly linear)
b
length normalization, typically 0.75 (0 means none, 1 means full)

Each piece, derived

  • idf: the base form ln((N - n + 0.5)/(n + 0.5)) is the log-odds that a random document does NOT contain t (with 0.5 smoothing). That form goes negative when n > N/2, so matching a very common word would lower the score. Adding 1 inside the log keeps idf positive. Example: N = 3, n = 2: classic idf is ln(1.5/2.5) = -0.511, while the +1 form gives ln(1 + 0.6) = 0.470.
  • Saturation: f(k1+1)/(f + K) equals 1 at f = 1 when K = k1 (an average-length doc) and approaches k1 + 1 = 2.2 as f grows. The first occurrence matters most.
  • Length normalization: K = k1(1 - b + b|D|/avgdl). A doc twice the average length has a bigger K, so the same f counts less.

Worked example on a tiny corpus

Docs: D1 "Cats chase mice." D2 "Dogs chase cats, and cats run." D3 "Mice eat cheese." Query: "cats mice"
  1. Tokens (lowercase, runs of [a-z0-9]): D1 = [cats, chase, mice] (3), D2 = [dogs, chase, cats, and, cats, run] (6), D3 = [mice, eat, cheese] (3). N = 3, avgdl = 12/3 = 4.
  2. Document frequencies: cats is in D1 and D2 (n = 2), mice is in D1 and D3 (n = 2). idf = ln(1 + (3 - 2 + 0.5)/(2 + 0.5)) = ln(1.6) = 0.470 for both.
  3. Length factor K = 1.2 x (0.25 + 0.75 x |D|/4): D1 and D3 (|D| = 3): K = 0.975. D2 (|D| = 6): K = 1.65.
  4. D1: cats f = 1: 1 x 2.2 / (1 + 0.975) = 1.1139. mice gives the same. Score = 0.470 x 1.1139 x 2 = 1.0471.
  5. D2: cats f = 2: 2 x 2.2 / (2 + 1.65) = 1.2055. No mice. Score = 0.470 x 1.2055 = 0.5666.
  6. D3: mice f = 1: 1.1139. Score = 0.470 x 1.1139 = 0.5235.
Ranking: D1 (1.0471), D2 (0.5666), D3 (0.5235)

Look at D2: "cats" twice in a 6-token doc only barely beats "mice" once in a 3-token doc. Saturation plus length normalization at work. Matching two different query terms (D1) roughly doubles the score.

import math
idf = math.log(1 + (3 - 2 + 0.5) / (2 + 0.5))          # 0.4700
K = lambda dl: 1.2 * (1 - 0.75 + 0.75 * dl / 4)
tf = lambda f, dl: f * 2.2 / (f + K(dl))
print(2 * idf * tf(1, 3), idf * tf(2, 6), idf * tf(1, 3))  # 1.0471 0.5666 0.5235

Common traps

  • Counting a repeated query term twice. In the coding problem's spec each distinct term counts once.
  • Computing avgdl only over matching docs. It is the mean over ALL docs.
  • Expecting stemming: "retrieved" does not match "retrieval" unless you add a stemmer.
  • Using n = number of occurrences across the corpus. It is the number of documents containing the term.
  • Forgetting that idf with +1 is always positive, so any match gives a score above 0.

How this gets asked

In BM25, what does the parameter b control?

  • Term frequency saturation That is k1.
  • How strongly document length is normalized Correct: b = 0 means none, b = 1 means full normalization by |D|/avgdl.
  • The idf smoothing constant That is the 0.5.
  • The number of results returned Unrelated.

k1 controls saturation and b controls length normalization. Defaults are k1 = 1.2 to 2.0 and b = 0.75.

k1 = 1.2, b = 0.75, a doc of exactly average length, term count f = 2. What is the term-frequency part f(k1+1)/(f + k1(1 - b + b|D|/avgdl)), before idf?

|D| = avgdl gives K = 1.2. So 2 x 2.2 / (2 + 1.2) = 4.4 / 3.2 = 1.375.

Self-check

Write the BM25 idf with +1.

ln(1 + (N - n + 0.5)/(n + 0.5)).

What value does the tf part approach as f grows?

k1 + 1 (2.2 for k1 = 1.2).

Why can the classic idf be negative, and why is that bad?

When n > N/2 the ratio is below 1, so the log is negative. Matching a common word would then decrease the score.

Reciprocal Rank Fusion and hybrid search

Intuition

You have two ranked lists, BM25 and dense, whose scores are on incompatible scales (BM25 is unbounded, cosine runs from -1 to 1). Instead of calibrating scores, RRF uses only ranks: being near the top of any list earns points, and appearing in several lists adds up. k = 60 dampens the gap between rank 1 and rank 2, so agreement between lists beats one list's strong opinion.

The formula

RRF (Cormack, Clarke and Buettcher, 2009)
RRF(d) = sum over lists L containing d of 1 / (k + rank_L(d))
rank_L(d)
1-based position of d in list L (top result has rank 1)
k
constant, 60 by convention (from the original paper)

Worked example

BM25: [D3, D1, D7]. Dense: [D1, D9, D3]. k = 60
  1. D1: rank 2 in BM25, rank 1 in dense: 1/62 + 1/61 = 0.03252.
  2. D3: rank 1 in BM25, rank 3 in dense: 1/61 + 1/63 = 0.03227.
  3. D9: dense rank 2 only: 1/62 = 0.01613.
  4. D7: BM25 rank 3 only: 1/63 = 0.01587.
Fused: D1, D3, D9, D7

Why k matters: with k = 0, rank 1 is worth twice rank 2 (1/1 vs 1/2). With k = 60 the ratio is (1/61) / (1/62) = 62/61 = 1.0164, almost flat. Documents found by both retrievers dominate.

Hybrid search in practice

  • Run BM25 and dense retrieval in parallel (each top 50 to 100), fuse with RRF, then rerank the top 20 to 50 with a cross-encoder.
  • Alternatives: weighted score fusion (alpha x normalized dense + (1 - alpha) x normalized BM25), which needs score normalization and tuning. RRF is the robust default.
  • Implementation: the code is in part 08.

Common traps

  • Using 0-based ranks (1/(60 + 0) for the top). The convention is 1-based.
  • Summing raw scores from different retrievers. That is not RRF.
  • Penalizing a doc missing from a list. It simply gets no contribution from that list.

How this gets asked

The document's 1-based rank in each list, and k = 60.

Self-check

Why does RRF not need score normalization?

It uses only ranks, never raw scores.

RRF contribution of a top-ranked doc with k = 60?

1/61, about 0.0164.

Effect of increasing k?

It flattens the difference between top ranks, giving more weight to appearing in multiple lists.

Ranking metrics with worked numbers

Intuition

Different metrics answer different questions. Did we find the relevant docs at all (recall)? How much junk came with them (precision)? How quickly does the first good one appear (MRR)? Are the best ones at the very top, with graded relevance (nDCG)?

Running example

One query. The system returns [d1, d2, d3, d4, d5]. Judgments (graded): d1 = 3, d3 = 2, d5 = 1, and d8 = 2, which was not retrieved. Everything else is 0. So 4 docs are relevant in total.

Definitions
precision@k = (# relevant in top k) / k recall@k = (# relevant in top k) / (total # relevant) hit@k = 1 if any relevant in top k else 0 (hit rate = mean over queries) RR = 1 / (rank of first relevant) (MRR = mean over queries) AP = (1 / total relevant) * sum over relevant hits of precision@(their rank) DCG@k = sum_{i=1..k} gain_i / log2(i + 1) nDCG@k = DCG@k / IDCG@k (IDCG = DCG of the ideal ordering of ALL judged docs)
Computed step by step
  1. Relevant in top 5: d1, d3, d5 = 3. precision@5 = 3/5 = 0.6. recall@5 = 3/4 = 0.75 (d8 missed).
  2. Top 3: d1, d3 relevant. precision@3 = 2/3. recall@3 = 2/4 = 0.5.
  3. hit@1 = 1 (d1 is relevant). RR = 1/1 = 1.
  4. AP: relevant hits at ranks 1, 3, 5 with precision 1/1, 2/3, 3/5. Sum = 2.2667. Divide by 4 (all relevant, including the missed d8): AP = 0.5667.
  5. DCG@5 with gain = relevance: 3/log2(2) + 0 + 2/log2(4) + 0 + 1/log2(6) = 3 + 1 + 0.3869 = 4.3869.
  6. Ideal order of all judged docs: 3, 2, 2, 1. IDCG@5 = 3/1 + 2/log2(3) + 2/log2(4) + 1/log2(5) = 3 + 1.2619 + 1 + 0.4307 = 5.6925.
  7. nDCG@5 = 4.3869 / 5.6925 = 0.7706.
P@5 0.6, R@5 0.75, RR 1, AP 0.5667, nDCG@5 0.7706

Add a second query whose only relevant doc is at rank 3: RR = 1/3 and AP = 1/3. Then MRR = (1 + 1/3)/2 = 0.6667 and MAP = (0.5667 + 0.3333)/2 = 0.45.

import math
dcg  = 3/math.log2(2) + 2/math.log2(4) + 1/math.log2(6)
idcg = 3/math.log2(2) + 2/math.log2(3) + 2/math.log2(4) + 1/math.log2(5)
print(round(dcg / idcg, 4))   # 0.7706

Chunking, near-duplicates and context packing

Chunking

  • Fixed-size with overlap: windows of size tokens, step = size - overlap. Number of chunks for n tokens is 1 + ceil((n - size)/step). For example, 10,000 tokens with size 512 and overlap 64 gives 23 chunks. Overlap keeps a sentence that straddles a boundary intact in at least one chunk.
  • Structure-aware: split on headings, paragraphs and tables first, then enforce a size limit. This usually beats blind fixed windows on documents with structure.
  • Small-to-big / parent document: index small chunks for precise matching, but return their parent section to the LLM for context.
  • Size tradeoff: small chunks give precise retrieval but little context. Large chunks give more context but diluted embeddings and more tokens.
  • Metadata: store the source, section title, date and access-control tags with each chunk for filtering and citation.

Near-duplicate removal

Jaccard similarity of word sets
J(A, B) = |A ∩ B| / |A ∪ B| integer test for J >= 0.8: 5 * |A ∩ B| >= 4 * |A ∪ B|
A, B
word (or shingle) sets of two chunks

"the cat sat on the mat" vs "the cat sat on a mat": sets {the, cat, sat, on, mat} and {the, cat, sat, on, a, mat}. Intersection 5, union 6, J = 0.833, so they are near-duplicates at a threshold of 0.8. The integer form avoids floating-point edge cases right at the threshold. Coding problem (a) uses exactly this test.

MinHash scales this to millions of docs. Apply a random hash to every element and keep the minimum. P(two sets share the min) = J. With 128 hash functions, the estimate's standard error at J = 0.8 is sqrt(0.8 x 0.2 / 128) = 0.035. LSH banding groups the signatures into bands so only likely pairs get compared, avoiding O(n^2) comparisons.

Context packing under a token budget

Given scored chunks and a budget: take chunks greedily by score, skip one that does not fit (but keep scanning, since a smaller chunk later might fit), and skip near-duplicates of chunks already taken. That is exactly coding problem (a). Greedy is not optimal for total score (that is a knapsack problem), but it is predictable and fast.

How this gets asked

Two chunks share 8 distinct words and their union has 11 distinct words. With a near-duplicate threshold of Jaccard 0.8, are they duplicates?

  • Yes, 8/11 rounds to 0.8 Never round at a threshold: 8/11 = 0.727.
  • No: 5 x 8 = 40 < 4 x 11 = 44 Correct. The integer test fails.
  • Yes, because more than half the words overlap The threshold is 0.8, not 0.5.
  • Cannot tell without embeddings Jaccard needs only the sets.

J = 0.727 < 0.8. With 10 shared out of 11 (the coding example), 50 >= 44 holds.

Self-check

Why use the integer test 5|A∩B| >= 4|A∪B|?

It is exact. Floating-point division can land a hair below 0.8 at the boundary.

What probability does MinHash estimate?

P(min-hashes equal) = Jaccard similarity.

What is parent-document retrieval?

Match on small chunks, but give the LLM the larger section they belong to.

ANN indexes and metadata filtering

Intuition

Exact nearest-neighbor search compares the query with every vector. That is fine for 100k vectors, slow for 100M. Approximate nearest neighbor (ANN) indexes trade a little recall for big speedups by only looking where the answer probably is.

Metadata filtering

  • Post-filtering (ANN first, then filter) can return fewer than k results when the filter is selective. Example: top 10 retrieved, 9 belong to another tenant, 1 result left.
  • Pre-filtering (filter, then search within the subset) is correct, but may fall back to brute force or need index support. Modern vector DBs integrate filters into the graph or cluster traversal.
  • Access control belongs in the filter (tenant_id, ACL tags), never in the prompt.

How this gets asked

HNSW recall is too low at the current latency budget. Which knob trades latency for recall at query time without rebuilding?

  • nlist nlist is an IVF build-time parameter.
  • M M is fixed at build time.
  • efSearch Correct: the size of the candidate list during search.
  • Embedding dimension That would need a new model and a re-embed.

efSearch (and nprobe for IVF) are the query-time recall and latency dials.

Self-check

HNSW vs IVF memory?

HNSW stores a graph plus full vectors, so it uses more memory. IVF (especially with PQ) is more compact.

Danger of post-filtering?

Selective filters leave fewer than k results.

When is flat search the right choice?

Small collections, and as ground truth when measuring ANN recall.

Basic What is RAG and why use it?

Retrieval-augmented generation: retrieve relevant documents for a query and include them in the prompt, so the model answers from current, private or citable knowledge instead of only its weights.

Basic What are the main components of a RAG pipeline?

Ingestion (parse, chunk, embed, index), retrieval (vector and/or keyword search), optional reranking, prompt construction with context, generation, and evaluation and monitoring.

Basic What is a vector database?

A store with approximate nearest-neighbor indexes (HNSW, IVF) for fast similarity search over embeddings, usually with metadata filtering.

Basic Why chunk documents, and what size?

Embeddings and context windows work better with focused passages. Typical chunks are a few hundred tokens with some overlap, ideally split on structure (headings, paragraphs). Tune by retrieval evals.

Basic Cosine similarity vs dot product?

Cosine normalizes by vector length. Dot product does not. After L2 normalization they rank identically. Use the metric the embedding model was trained for.

Medium Your recall@20 is 0.93 but answer accuracy is 0.61 and wrong chunks get quoted. What do you do?

Retrieval is fine and ranking or generation is the problem. Add a cross-encoder reranker and pass only the top 3 to 5 chunks. Do not raise temperature, retrieve more, or swap embeddings.

Medium Bi-encoder vs cross-encoder?

Bi-encoders embed query and document separately, so documents are precomputed and search is fast (first stage). Cross-encoders read query and document together, which is more accurate but needs one forward pass per pair (reranking).

Medium Explain BM25 and why hybrid search helps.

BM25 scores term matches with idf weighting, saturating term frequency (k1) and length normalization (b). Hybrid search fuses BM25 (exact terms, codes, names) with dense retrieval (semantics), usually via RRF.

Medium What is Reciprocal Rank Fusion?

Score = sum over ranked lists of 1/(k + rank) with 1-based rank and k = 60. It needs no score normalization, and items found by several retrievers rise to the top.

Medium What happens if you upgrade the embedding model?

Vectors from different models are incompatible even at the same dimension, so re-embed the entire corpus and the queries. Do a blue/green migration with dual writes and evaluate before switching.

Medium How do you evaluate a RAG system?

Retrieval metrics (recall@k, MRR, nDCG) on labeled query-to-document pairs, plus generation metrics: answer correctness, faithfulness or groundedness, citation precision, abstention. Measure each stage separately to localize failures.

Medium What is 'lost in the middle'?

LLMs use information at the start and end of a long context better than in the middle. Put the most relevant chunks first or last, and pass fewer, better chunks.

Advanced How do you enforce document-level permissions in RAG?

Store ACL metadata with each chunk and pre-filter retrieval by the user's groups (filter-aware ANN). Never rely on the prompt to hide content. Test with cross-permission probes.

Advanced HNSW vs IVF-PQ: when would you choose each?

HNSW: high recall and low latency, but memory-heavy and slower to build. Good up to tens of millions of vectors in RAM. IVF-PQ: compressed and memory-efficient for very large corpora, with lower recall, reranked with full vectors.

Advanced How would you handle multi-hop questions in RAG?

Query decomposition into sub-questions, iterative retrieval (retrieve, read, retrieve again), agentic RAG with a search tool, or graph-based retrieval over linked entities. Evaluate with multi-hop test sets.

Advanced What is the difference between post-filtering and pre-filtering in vector search?

Post-filtering runs ANN then applies metadata filters, which can return fewer than k results for selective filters. Pre-filtering restricts the candidate set first, which is correct but needs index support (filtered HNSW or partitioned indexes).
