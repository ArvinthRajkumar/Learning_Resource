NLP, tokenisation, embeddings and vector search

Language models turn text into numbers twice: first by splitting it into tokens, then by mapping tokens to vectors. This part builds both steps, then the machinery that searches millions of vectors fast.

MediumAdvanced~6 hours

From text to tokens

Why tokenisation exists

Models work on integers. Splitting text into words gives a huge vocabulary and cannot handle new words; splitting into characters is tiny but makes sequences very long. Subword tokenisation sits between: common words are single tokens, rare words are built from pieces, and any text can be represented (down to bytes).

Byte-pair encoding (BPE) from scratch

Start from characters. Repeatedly find the most frequent adjacent pair of symbols and merge it into a new symbol. The list of merges is the tokenizer; to encode new text, apply the merges in the order they were learned.

from collections import Counter

def learn_bpe(words, merges):
    vocab = Counter(tuple(w) + ("</w>",) for w in words)      # each word as a tuple of symbols
    learned = []
    for _ in range(merges):
        pairs = Counter()
        for word, freq in vocab.items():
            for a, b in zip(word, word[1:]):
                pairs[(a, b)] += freq
        if not pairs:
            break
        best = max(pairs, key=lambda p: (pairs[p], p))         # most frequent, ties broken deterministically
        learned.append(best)
        new_vocab = Counter()
        for word, freq in vocab.items():
            out, i = [], 0
            while i < len(word):
                if i < len(word) - 1 and (word[i], word[i + 1]) == best:
                    out.append(word[i] + word[i + 1]); i += 2
                else:
                    out.append(word[i]); i += 1
            new_vocab[tuple(out)] += freq
        vocab = new_vocab
    return learned

def encode(word, merges):
    symbols = list(word) + ["</w>"]
    for a, b in merges:
        i = 0
        while i < len(symbols) - 1:
            if symbols[i] == a and symbols[i + 1] == b:
                symbols[i:i + 2] = [a + b]
            else:
                i += 1
    return symbols

corpus = ["low"] * 5 + ["lower"] * 2 + ["newest"] * 6 + ["widest"] * 3
merges = learn_bpe(corpus, 10)
print(merges[:5])
print(encode("lowest", merges))
print(encode("newer", merges))

Output

[('t', '</w>'), ('s', 't</w>'), ('e', 'st</w>'), ('o', 'w'), ('l', 'ow')]
['low', 'est</w>']
['ne', 'w', 'e', 'r', '</w>']

Words that were seen often (low, newest) become one or two tokens; the unseen word lowest is assembled from known pieces. Real tokenizers (GPT, LLaMA) run BPE on bytes so nothing is out of vocabulary, with vocabularies of 32k to 200k and special tokens (<|endoftext|>, chat markers).

Classical text representations

Preprocessing

Lowercase, strip punctuation, split into tokens, remove stop words, stem or lemmatise. Modern neural models mostly skip heavy preprocessing; classical models rely on it.

Bag of words and TF-IDF

A bag of words counts how often each term appears and ignores order. TF-IDF reweights: term frequency (how often a term appears in the document) times inverse document frequency log(N / df) (rare terms are more informative).

import math
docs = ["the cat sat on the mat", "the dog sat on the log", "cats and dogs are pets"]
tokens = [d.split() for d in docs]
N = len(docs)
vocab = sorted({w for t in tokens for w in t})
df = {w: sum(w in t for t in tokens) for w in vocab}
def tfidf(t):
    return {w: (t.count(w) / len(t)) * math.log(N / df[w]) for w in sorted(set(t))}
for d, t in zip(docs, tokens):
    top = sorted(tfidf(t).items(), key=lambda kv: (-round(kv[1], 6), kv[0]))[:3]
    print(d, "->", [(w, round(s, 2)) for w, s in top])

Output

the cat sat on the mat -> [('cat', 0.18), ('mat', 0.18), ('the', 0.14)]
the dog sat on the log -> [('dog', 0.18), ('log', 0.18), ('the', 0.14)]
cats and dogs are pets -> [('and', 0.22), ('are', 0.22), ('cats', 0.22)]

The word the appears everywhere and scores low; distinctive words score high. TF-IDF vectors with cosine similarity were the backbone of search for decades, and BM25 refines it with term-frequency saturation and length normalisation (03). Limits: no semantics (car and automobile are unrelated), huge sparse vectors.

How this gets asked

Why does TF-IDF weight rare terms more?

  • Inverse document frequency makes terms appearing in few documents more informative Correct.
  • They are stop words No.
  • It is a bug No.

A word in every document cannot discriminate between documents.

Embeddings

The idea

An embedding maps a token (or sentence, image...) to a dense vector, usually 128 to 4,096 numbers, so that similar things are close. It is a learned lookup table: row i of a matrix holds the vector for token i.

Word2vec in one paragraph

The distributional hypothesis: words that appear in similar contexts have similar meanings. Skip-gram trains a model to predict neighbouring words from a centre word. As a by-product the input vectors capture meaning, giving relations like king - man + woman close to queen. Training uses negative sampling: raise the score of real (word, context) pairs, lower it for random pairs.

import random, math
random.seed(0)
sentences = ["cats like milk", "dogs like bones", "cats chase mice", "dogs chase cats", "kings rule lands", "queens rule lands"]
vocab = sorted({w for s in sentences for w in s.split()})
idx = {w: i for i, w in enumerate(vocab)}
dim = 8
W = [[random.uniform(-0.5, 0.5) for _ in range(dim)] for _ in vocab]      # centre vectors
C = [[random.uniform(-0.5, 0.5) for _ in range(dim)] for _ in vocab]      # context vectors
sig = lambda z: 1 / (1 + math.exp(-z))
pairs = []
for s in sentences:
    ws = s.split()
    for i, w in enumerate(ws):
        for j in range(max(0, i - 1), min(len(ws), i + 2)):
            if i != j:
                pairs.append((idx[w], idx[ws[j]]))
for epoch in range(400):
    for c, o in pairs:
        for target, label in ((o, 1), (random.randrange(len(vocab)), 0)):      # one positive, one random negative
            score = sum(W[c][k] * C[target][k] for k in range(dim))
            g = (sig(score) - label) * 0.1
            for k in range(dim):
                wk, ck = W[c][k], C[target][k]
                W[c][k] -= g * ck; C[target][k] -= g * wk
cos = lambda a, b: sum(x * y for x, y in zip(a, b)) / math.sqrt(sum(x * x for x in a) * sum(y * y for y in b))
for a, b in (("cats", "dogs"), ("kings", "queens"), ("cats", "kings")):
    print("%-6s %-7s similarity %.2f" % (a, b, cos(W[idx[a]], W[idx[b]])))

Output

cats   dogs    similarity 0.99
kings  queens  similarity 0.96
cats   kings   similarity 0.32

cats and dogs share contexts (like, chase), as do kings and queens, so each pair ends up more similar to each other than to unrelated words. This is the core of every embedding method.

Sentence and document embeddings

Retrieval and RAG need a vector for a whole passage. Approaches: average word vectors (weak baseline); use the [CLS] token or mean-pooled token vectors of a transformer; and, best, models trained for the task (Sentence-BERT, E5, BGE, OpenAI and Cohere embedding models).

Contrastive training

Train an encoder so that matching pairs (a question and its answer; an image and its caption) have high similarity and non-matching pairs low. With a batch of B pairs, the other B - 1 items in the batch serve as negatives and the loss is cross-entropy over the similarity matrix (InfoNCE).

import math
sim = [[0.9, 0.2, 0.1],      # row i: similarity of query i to documents 0..2 (document i is the match)
       [0.3, 0.8, 0.2],
       [0.1, 0.4, 0.7]]
temp = 0.1
loss = 0
for i, row in enumerate(sim):
    logits = [s / temp for s in row]
    m = max(logits)
    lse = m + math.log(sum(math.exp(l - m) for l in logits))
    loss += -(logits[i] - lse)                # -log softmax at the correct document
print("InfoNCE loss: %.4f" % (loss / len(sim)))

Output

InfoNCE loss: 0.0205

Hard negatives (confusing non-matches) and large batches make the model sharper. Matryoshka embeddings are trained so that the first k dimensions are usable alone, allowing cheaper truncated vectors.

Choosing and using embeddings

  • Pick by your task and language; check a retrieval benchmark such as MTEB, then test on your data.
  • Normalise vectors to unit length so cosine similarity equals the dot product.
  • Embed queries and documents with the same model and its recommended prefixes.
  • Chunk long documents sensibly (A12); embedding models have a maximum input length.
  • Changing the embedding model means re-embedding everything: version your indexes.

Vector search at scale

The problem

Given a query vector, find the nearest k among millions. Exact search compares to every vector: O(N d) per query. For 100 million vectors of 768 dimensions that is 77 billion multiply-adds, too slow, so we use approximate nearest neighbour (ANN) search, trading a little recall for large speedups.

import random, math
random.seed(1)
d, N = 16, 3000
data = [[random.gauss(0, 1) for _ in range(d)] for _ in range(N)]
q = [random.gauss(0, 1) for _ in range(d)]
dist = lambda a, b: math.dist(a, b)
exact = sorted(range(N), key=lambda i: dist(q, data[i]))[:5]
print("exact top-5:", exact, "(comparisons:", N, ")")

Output

exact top-5: [2372, 692, 2774, 1848, 1629] (comparisons: 3000 )

Locality-sensitive hashing: random hyperplanes

Pick random vectors; the sign of the dot product with each gives one bit. Nearby vectors agree on most bits, so they land in the same bucket. The check below measures how many true neighbours share the query's bucket.

import random, math
random.seed(2)
d, N, bits = 16, 3000, 10
data = [[random.gauss(0, 1) for _ in range(d)] for _ in range(N)]
planes = [[random.gauss(0, 1) for _ in range(d)] for _ in range(bits)]
sig = lambda v: tuple(sum(a * b for a, b in zip(p, v)) > 0 for p in planes)
buckets = {}
for i, v in enumerate(data):
    buckets.setdefault(sig(v), []).append(i)
q = [x + random.gauss(0, 0.2) for x in data[0]]            # a query close to data[0]
cands = buckets.get(sig(q), [])
best = min(cands, key=lambda i: math.dist(q, data[i])) if cands else None
print("candidates checked:", len(cands), "of", N, "| found the true neighbour:", best == 0)

Output

candidates checked: 3 of 3000 | found the true neighbour: True

Only a few dozen candidates are compared instead of thousands.

Recall, latency and memory

  • Recall@k: the fraction of the true top-k that the ANN index returns. Tune index parameters (nprobe, efSearch) to a recall target (for example 95 percent) and measure latency at that point.
  • Memory: 100 million vectors of 768 float32 values is 307 GB raw; PQ or int8 quantisation or Matryoshka truncation cuts it by 4x to 64x.
  • Filtering by metadata (tenant, date) is tricky in graph indexes: pre-filter, post-filter, or use filtered-HNSW; test recall under filters.
  • Hybrid search combines keyword (BM25) and vector results with reciprocal rank fusion (03); reranking with a cross-encoder improves the top results.

Practical rules: build a small, representative, labelled evaluation set early; read errors by hand (error analysis finds more than metrics); split by document or user to avoid leakage; track slices (language, length, topic); compare against a simple baseline such as TF-IDF plus logistic regression.

Key idea

Tokenisation fixes what the model can see; embeddings fix what it considers similar; the index fixes how fast you can find it. Weakness in any of the three limits the whole system, so measure each separately.

Interview questions: NLP and embeddings

12 questions. Try to answer aloud in under a minute, then reveal. Tags show the level.

Basic What is tokenisation and why subwords?

Splitting text into units the model processes. Subwords balance vocabulary size and sequence length and handle unseen words by composing known pieces.

Basic TF-IDF in one sentence?

A term's weight is its frequency in the document times the log of how rare it is across documents, so distinctive words score high.

Basic What is an embedding?

A dense learned vector for a token or item such that similar things are close together.

Medium Describe how BPE is trained.

Start from characters, repeatedly merge the most frequent adjacent pair into a new symbol, and record the merges; encoding applies the merges in order.

Medium How does word2vec learn?

Skip-gram predicts context words from a centre word, using negative sampling to push real pairs together and random pairs apart; words sharing contexts end up with similar vectors.

Medium Static versus contextual embeddings?

Static embeddings give one vector per word type; contextual embeddings (BERT, GPT hidden states) give different vectors depending on the surrounding words.

Medium How are text embedding models trained?

Contrastively: matching pairs (question and answer, query and document) are pulled together and in-batch or hard negatives pushed apart using an InfoNCE loss.

Medium Why normalise embeddings?

Unit-length vectors make cosine similarity equal the dot product and remove length effects, simplifying and speeding search.

Advanced Explain IVF, PQ and HNSW.

IVF clusters vectors and searches the nearest clusters; PQ compresses vectors into short codes; HNSW is a layered neighbour graph searched greedily, giving the best recall-latency trade-off at higher memory.

Advanced How do you evaluate an ANN index?

Recall@k against exact search at a given latency and memory, tuning parameters like nprobe or efSearch to a target recall, and testing under metadata filters.

Advanced Why can LLMs fail at character-level tasks?

They process subword tokens rather than characters, so letter-level structure is hidden inside tokens.

Advanced How would you choose an embedding model?

Shortlist by benchmark (MTEB) and language, test recall@k on your own labelled queries, consider dimension, cost, latency and max length, and plan for re-embedding when you change models.
