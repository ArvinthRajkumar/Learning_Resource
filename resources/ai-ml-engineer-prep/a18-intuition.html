Part A18

Machine learning and LLMs explained simply: analogies, pictures and the questions beginners ask

Before the equations comes the mental picture. This page gives one analogy per core idea in machine learning and large language models, shows a tiny experiment that makes it concrete, and ends each with a model answer you can say in 30 seconds.

BasicMedium~4 hours

How to use this page

Read the picture, run the experiment, then cover the answer and say it aloud. Good explanations name the idea, the problem it solves and the trade-off. The goal is to be understood by someone smart who has not studied the topic.

Learning from data

What "learning" means

Picture: a student learning to grade essays by seeing thousands of graded examples, instead of being handed a rulebook. The model adjusts internal knobs (parameters) until its grades match the examples. The goal is to grade new essays well, not to remember the old ones. Answer: "A model is a function with adjustable parameters. Training changes the parameters to reduce an error measured on examples, hoping the pattern generalises to unseen data."

Overfitting and underfitting

Picture: a student who memorises last year's exam answers (overfit: perfect on the old paper, lost on a new one) versus a student who only learned the chapter titles (underfit: bad on both). The aim is to understand the ideas. Experiment: fit curves of increasing flexibility to noisy data and compare error on training points and on new points.

import random
random.seed(1)
def true_f(x): return 2 * x + 1
xs = [i / 10 for i in range(0, 11)]
train = [(x, true_f(x) + random.gauss(0, 0.3)) for x in xs]
test = [(x + 0.05, true_f(x + 0.05) + random.gauss(0, 0.3)) for x in xs[:-1]]
def fit_poly(points, degree):                                 # least squares by solving the normal equations
    n = degree + 1
    A = [[sum(x ** (i + j) for x, _ in points) for j in range(n)] for i in range(n)]
    b = [sum(y * x ** i for x, y in points) for i in range(n)]
    M = [A[i] + [b[i]] for i in range(n)]
    for i in range(n):
        p = max(range(i, n), key=lambda r: abs(M[r][i])); M[i], M[p] = M[p], M[i]
        for r in range(i + 1, n):
            f = M[r][i] / M[i][i]; M[r] = [a - f * c for a, c in zip(M[r], M[i])]
    coef = [0.0] * n
    for i in reversed(range(n)): coef[i] = (M[i][n] - sum(M[i][j] * coef[j] for j in range(i + 1, n))) / M[i][i]
    return coef
predict = lambda c, x: sum(ci * x ** i for i, ci in enumerate(c))
mse = lambda c, pts: sum((predict(c, x) - y) ** 2 for x, y in pts) / len(pts)
for degree in (0, 1, 3, 8):
    c = fit_poly(train, degree)
    print(f"degree {degree}: training error {mse(c, train):.3f} | error on new points {mse(c, test):.3f}")

Output

degree 0: training error 0.366 | error on new points 0.616
degree 1: training error 0.066 | error on new points 0.130
degree 3: training error 0.021 | error on new points 0.181
degree 8: training error 0.004 | error on new points 0.238

The flexible degree-8 curve chases the noise (tiny training error, but it wobbles between points), a flat line misses the trend, and the straight line, matching the true pattern, does best on new points.

Bias and variance

Picture: archery. Bias is aiming consistently at the wrong spot (the model is too simple to hit the target). Variance is a shaky hand: the arrows scatter, and a different handful of training examples would give a different model. Simpler models have high bias and low variance; flexible models the reverse. More data reduces variance; a better model family reduces bias.

Train, validation and test sets

Picture: practice problems (train), a mock exam you use to decide how to revise (validation), and the real exam you see once (test). If you keep adjusting after peeking at the real exam, your score no longer measures what you can do on a fresh one.

Regularisation

Picture: a budget or a penalty for complexity, like a rule that essays must be short, so the model prefers simple explanations unless the data really demands more. L2 shrinks all weights a bit; L1 pushes some to exactly zero (feature selection); dropout randomly silences parts of a network so none can be relied on too much; early stopping ends training when practice scores stop improving.

How this gets asked

What does overfitting mean?

  • The model is too small That is underfitting.
  • The model fits noise in the training data and performs worse on new data Correct.
  • Training takes too long No.
  • The data is too large No.

Detect it by the gap between training and validation error.

Why keep a separate test set that you look at only once?

  • To save compute No.
  • So its score measures performance on genuinely unseen data Correct.
  • To increase training data No.
  • Because tests are small No.

Tuning against the test set leaks information into your choices.

Self-check

Explain bias and variance with archery.

Bias is aiming consistently at the wrong spot; variance is scattered shots from an unsteady hand; flexible models trade bias for variance.

How models improve: gradients

Gradient descent

Picture: walking down a foggy hill by feeling which way the ground slopes under your feet and stepping downhill. The gradient is the slope; the learning rate is your step size. Too big and you overshoot or bounce; too small and it takes forever; the loss is your height above the valley floor.

def descend(lr, steps=20):
    x = 5.0                                                    # minimise f(x) = x^2 whose gradient is 2x
    for _ in range(steps): x -= lr * 2 * x
    return x
for lr in (0.01, 0.1, 0.5, 1.1):
    print(f"learning rate {lr:>4}: after 20 steps x = {descend(lr):12.4f}", "(diverged)" if abs(descend(lr)) > 5 else "")

Output

learning rate 0.01: after 20 steps x =       3.3380 
learning rate  0.1: after 20 steps x =       0.0576 
learning rate  0.5: after 20 steps x =       0.0000 
learning rate  1.1: after 20 steps x =     191.6880 (diverged)

Backpropagation

Picture: a factory assembly line that produces a faulty product, and a manager tracing blame backward station by station: how much did each station's setting contribute to the final defect? The chain rule passes the blame backwards, and each parameter gets the exact amount by which nudging it would change the loss.

Stochastic gradient descent and batches

Picture: estimating which way the hill slopes by testing a few random patches rather than surveying the entire mountain every step. It is noisier but much cheaper, and the noise can even help escape poor spots.

Why deep networks work

Picture: a pipeline of simple filters. Early layers detect edges, middle layers combine edges into shapes, later layers recognise objects. Each layer re-describes the input in a more useful way: representation learning.

How this gets asked

In gradient descent, what does a too-large learning rate cause?

  • Slow but steady progress That is too small.
  • Overshooting and possible divergence Correct.
  • Perfect convergence No.
  • Overfitting only No.

Step size must match the curvature of the loss.

Self-check

Explain gradient descent in one sentence.

Repeatedly step the parameters in the direction that most reduces the loss, with step size set by the learning rate.

Measuring quality

Accuracy, precision and recall

Picture: a fishing net. Recall is how many of the fish in the lake you caught; precision is how much of your catch is fish rather than boots. A cancer screening wants high recall (miss no one) and tolerates false alarms; a spam filter wants high precision (never bin a real email).

actual    = [1] * 10 + [0] * 990                              # 1% of cases are positive
lazy      = [0] * 1000                                         # a model that always says "negative"
decent    = [1] * 7 + [0] * 3 + [1] * 20 + [0] * 970           # finds 7 of 10, raises 20 false alarms
def scores(pred):
    tp = sum(p and a for p, a in zip(pred, actual)); fp = sum(p and not a for p, a in zip(pred, actual)); fn = sum(a and not p for p, a in zip(pred, actual))
    acc = sum(p == a for p, a in zip(pred, actual)) / len(actual)
    return acc, tp / max(1, tp + fp), tp / max(1, tp + fn)
for name, pred in [("always 'no'", lazy), ("a real model", decent)]:
    a, p, r = scores(pred); print(f"{name:>12}: accuracy {a:.1%}, precision {p:.1%}, recall {r:.1%}")

Output

 always 'no': accuracy 99.0%, precision 0.0%, recall 0.0%
a real model: accuracy 97.7%, precision 25.9%, recall 70.0%

The model that never finds anything is "99% accurate". That is why accuracy alone misleads on imbalanced data.

Cross-validation

Picture: rotating who sits the exam. Split the data into folds; each fold takes a turn as the test set while the others train; average the scores. You get a steadier estimate than one lucky or unlucky split.

How this gets asked

Why can a model that always predicts 'no' have 99% accuracy?

  • Accuracy is wrong It is correct but uninformative.
  • The positive class is rare, so most predictions are trivially right; precision and recall expose it Correct.
  • Bug No.
  • It is overfit No.

Use metrics that fit the class balance and costs.

Self-check

Precision versus recall in a fishing net analogy?

Recall is the share of all fish you caught; precision is the share of your catch that is fish.

Representations: embeddings and attention

Embeddings

Picture: a map where every word, product or image has coordinates, and things with similar meaning sit near each other. "King" and "queen" are close; "banana" is far away. The model learns the coordinates, and directions can even carry meaning. Searching by meaning is just finding the nearest points.

import math
vec = {"king": (0.9, 0.8), "queen": (0.85, 0.75), "man": (0.8, 0.3), "woman": (0.75, 0.25), "banana": (-0.5, 0.1)}
cos = lambda a, b: sum(x * y for x, y in zip(a, b)) / (math.hypot(*a) * math.hypot(*b))
for a, b in [("king", "queen"), ("man", "woman"), ("king", "banana")]: print(f"similarity({a}, {b}) = {cos(vec[a], vec[b]):+.2f}")

Output

similarity(king, queen) = +1.00
similarity(man, woman) = +1.00
similarity(king, banana) = -0.60

Tokens

Picture: Lego bricks. A language model does not read letters or whole words but chunks ("un", "believ", "able"), common pieces that make the vocabulary manageable. Rare or foreign words split into many small bricks, costing more tokens (and money). Roughly 4 characters or three-quarters of a word per token in English.

Attention

Picture: reading a sentence and, for each word, glancing back at the other words that matter for understanding it. In "The animal didn't cross the street because it was tired", the word "it" attends strongly to "animal". Attention computes how much each word should listen to each other word, then mixes their information accordingly. Cost grows with the square of the text length, since everyone considers everyone.

import math
words = ["the", "animal", "was", "tired"]
scores = {"the": [1, 0, 0, 0], "animal": [0.2, 1, 0, 0], "was": [0, 0.8, 1, 0], "tired": [0, 2.0, 0.3, 1]}      # hand-made similarity scores for illustration
def softmax(z): e = [math.exp(v) for v in z]; s = sum(e); return [v / s for v in e]
w = softmax(scores["tired"])
print("when processing 'tired', attention weights over", words, "=", [round(v, 2) for v in w], "-> it listens most to 'animal'")

Output

when processing 'tired', attention weights over ['the', 'animal', 'was', 'tired'] = [0.08, 0.59, 0.11, 0.22] -> it listens most to 'animal'

Transformers

Picture: a team of readers. Every layer, all words look at each other at the same time (attention), then each word thinks privately (a small feed-forward network), and the result goes to the next layer. Because everything is processed in parallel, transformers train quickly on GPUs, unlike the older word-by-word recurrent networks.

How this gets asked

What does an embedding place close together?

  • Words with the same spelling No.
  • Items with similar meaning or use Correct.
  • Items of equal length No.
  • Random items No.

Nearest-neighbour search then finds related items.

Why does attention cost grow quickly with text length?

  • It reads slowly No.
  • Every token attends to every other token, so cost grows with the square of the length Correct.
  • It stores images No.
  • It uses recursion No.

That is why long-context research focuses on efficiency.

Large language models

What a language model does

Picture: autocomplete on a vast scale. Given the text so far, predict a probability for each possible next token; pick one; append it; repeat. Everything an LLM writes is produced one token at a time, which is why temperature (how adventurous the pick is) changes style: low is predictable, high is creative and error-prone.

import math, random
random.seed(5)
logits = {"cat": 3.0, "dog": 2.5, "pizza": 0.5, "the": -1.0}
def sample(temp):
    scaled = {w: v / temp for w, v in logits.items()}; m = max(scaled.values())
    exp = {w: math.exp(v - m) for w, v in scaled.items()}; s = sum(exp.values())
    probs = {w: e / s for w, e in exp.items()}
    return probs, random.choices(list(probs), weights=list(probs.values()))[0]
for t in (0.2, 1.0, 2.0):
    probs, pick = sample(t); print(f"temperature {t}: probabilities {({w: round(p, 2) for w, p in probs.items()})}")

Output

temperature 0.2: probabilities {'cat': 0.92, 'dog': 0.08, 'pizza': 0.0, 'the': 0.0}
temperature 1.0: probabilities {'cat': 0.59, 'dog': 0.36, 'pizza': 0.05, 'the': 0.01}
temperature 2.0: probabilities {'cat': 0.45, 'dog': 0.35, 'pizza': 0.13, 'the': 0.06}

Pre-training, fine-tuning and alignment

Picture: a person's education. Pre-training is reading a huge library to learn language and facts (expensive, done once). Supervised fine-tuning is apprenticeship: learning to follow instructions from worked examples. Preference tuning (RLHF, DPO) is feedback from a mentor on which of two answers is better, shaping helpfulness and safety.

Context window

Picture: the model's desk. Only what is on the desk right now (the prompt plus the conversation so far) can be used; there is no memory outside it. A bigger desk holds more documents but costs more to search and can make the model overlook things in the middle.

Hallucination

Picture: a confident improviser. The model's training objective is to produce plausible text, not verified truth, so when it does not know, it can still produce a fluent, wrong answer. Mitigations: ground answers in retrieved sources, ask for citations, lower temperature, add checks, and make "I don't know" an acceptable answer.

RAG (retrieval-augmented generation)

Picture: an open-book exam. Instead of relying on memory, the system first looks up relevant passages in your documents (by meaning, using embeddings) and places them in the prompt, then the model answers from them. It keeps knowledge fresh and private without retraining, and lets you cite sources.

Fine-tuning and LoRA

Picture: instead of rewriting a whole textbook to teach a new skill, attach a few sticky notes to the pages that need adjusting. LoRA freezes the original weights and trains small low-rank add-on matrices (often under 1% of the parameters), so fine-tuning needs far less memory and the add-ons are small files you can swap.

Quantisation

Picture: rounding prices to the nearest whole unit to save space on the price list. Storing weights with 8 or 4 bits instead of 16 shrinks the model 2 to 4 times and speeds up loading, at a small cost in accuracy.

weights = [0.113, -0.482, 0.907, -0.031, 0.356, -0.774]
def quantize(ws, bits):
    levels = 2 ** (bits - 1) - 1; scale = max(abs(w) for w in ws) / levels
    return [round(w / scale) * scale for w in ws]
for bits in (8, 4, 2):
    q = quantize(weights, bits); err = max(abs(a - b) for a, b in zip(weights, q))
    print(f"{bits} bits ({2 ** bits} levels): max rounding error {err:.3f}")

Output

8 bits (256 levels): max rounding error 0.004
4 bits (16 levels): max rounding error 0.036
2 bits (4 levels): max rounding error 0.425

Agents and tools

Picture: a clever assistant who can also use a phone and a calculator. The model decides which tool to call (search, code, database), reads the result, and continues, looping until done. More capability, more ways to go wrong, so give narrow permissions, validate inputs and keep a human in the loop for risky actions.

How this gets asked

What does a higher sampling temperature do?

  • Makes the output deterministic That is low temperature.
  • Flattens the probabilities so less likely tokens are chosen more often, giving more varied (and riskier) output Correct.
  • Speeds up inference No.
  • Adds more facts No.

Use low temperature for factual tasks.

What problem does RAG address?

  • Slow GPUs No.
  • The model lacks current or private knowledge and may hallucinate; retrieval supplies relevant passages in the prompt Correct.
  • Large file sizes No.
  • Bad tokenisation No.

It keeps knowledge outside the weights, so it can be updated and cited.

Self-check

What is the difference between pre-training and fine-tuning?

Pre-training learns general language and knowledge from huge raw text; fine-tuning adapts the model with smaller curated data to follow instructions or a domain.

Why does hallucination happen?

The model is trained to produce plausible continuations, not verified facts, so it can fluently fill gaps with wrong content.

Questions beginners ask

QuestionShort answer
Does the model "understand"?It captures statistical structure well enough to be useful and sometimes to reason; whether that is understanding is philosophical. Test behaviour, not metaphors.
Why does it make up facts?It is trained to continue text plausibly, not to check facts; without grounding it fills gaps fluently.
Why is more data usually better?It reduces variance and exposes rare cases, if the data is relevant and clean. Quality beats quantity at the margin.
Why do GPUs matter?Neural networks are mostly large matrix multiplications, which GPUs perform in parallel thousands of times faster than CPUs.
Why is training expensive but use cheap?Training does a forward and backward pass over trillions of tokens; use does only a forward pass per token. Serving cost is still large at scale.
What is a parameter?A number inside the model that training adjusts; a 7B model has seven billion of them.
Is a bigger model always better?Not always: data, training length, architecture and fine-tuning matter, and smaller models are cheaper and faster.
Why test on unseen data?Performance on training data measures memory; performance on unseen data measures learning.
Can I trust the output?Verify anything important; measure accuracy on your own task with an evaluation set.
Key idea

Models are adjustable functions fitted to examples; the danger is memorising rather than generalising (overfitting), controlled with held-out data, regularisation and simpler models. Gradients tell each parameter which way to move; embeddings place meaning on a map; attention lets every token consult the others; LLMs predict one token at a time and are shaped by pre-training, fine-tuning and feedback. Ground them with retrieval, evaluate on your own data, and verify.

Interview questions: Intuition guide

14 questions. Try to answer aloud in under a minute, then reveal. Tags show the level.

Basic Explain overfitting simply.

The model memorises the training examples, including their noise, so it performs well on them and poorly on new data, like a student who memorised last year's answers.

Basic Explain gradient descent simply.

Walk downhill on the loss by repeatedly stepping the parameters in the direction that reduces the error, with the learning rate setting the step size.

Basic Explain embeddings simply.

A map where items with similar meaning are close together, so similarity search is finding nearby points.

Basic Explain precision and recall simply.

Precision is how much of what you flagged is correct; recall is how much of the real positives you found.

Medium Explain attention simply.

For each token, decide how much to listen to every other token and mix their information accordingly, with cost growing with the square of the length.

Medium Explain bias and variance simply.

Bias is consistently missing the target because the model is too simple; variance is scattered answers because the model is too sensitive to the particular training data.

Medium Why do LLMs hallucinate?

They are trained to produce plausible continuations, not to verify facts, so they can fluently fill gaps with wrong information.

Medium Explain RAG simply.

An open-book exam: retrieve relevant documents by meaning and put them in the prompt so the model answers from them.

Medium Explain LoRA simply.

Freeze the base weights and train small low-rank add-on matrices, like sticky notes, so fine-tuning needs little memory and the adapters are small files.

Medium Explain quantisation simply.

Store weights with fewer bits, like rounding prices, shrinking the model and speeding loading at a small accuracy cost.

Medium What does temperature do?

It rescales the next-token probabilities: low temperature makes the model pick likely tokens, high temperature flattens the distribution for more varied output.

Advanced Explain pre-training, fine-tuning and RLHF as an education.

Pre-training is reading a vast library, supervised fine-tuning is apprenticeship from worked examples, preference tuning is a mentor's feedback on which answers are better.

Advanced Why can accuracy be misleading?

On imbalanced data a model that always predicts the majority class scores high accuracy while finding nothing; use precision, recall, PR curves or cost-based metrics.

Advanced How would you explain a transformer to a manager?

A stack of layers where every word looks at every other word to understand context, then thinks about it privately; processing everything in parallel makes it fast to train and powerful.
