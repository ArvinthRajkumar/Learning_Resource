Evaluation, data, safety and ethics of image generators

A model that produces beautiful images can still be wrong in ways a single sample hides. This part covers how to measure quality and diversity honestly, how data shapes behaviour, and the safety and legal questions every practitioner is asked.

MediumAdvanced~4 hours

Measuring generative models

Why it is hard

There is no single correct output, so we compare distributions: generated images against real ones, and generated images against prompts. We care about three things at once: fidelity (each sample looks real), diversity (samples cover the data), and alignment (the image matches the prompt). One number cannot capture all three.

Fréchet Inception Distance (FID)

Pass real and generated images through a pretrained Inception network, take the penultimate features, fit a Gaussian to each set, and measure the Fréchet distance between the two Gaussians. Lower is better.

FID
FID = norm(mu_r - mu_g)^2 + Tr( S_r + S_g - 2 (S_r S_g)^(1/2) )
mu
mean of the features
S
covariance of the features
r
real images
g
generated images

In one dimension the covariances are variances and the formula becomes (mu_r - mu_g)^2 + (s_r - s_g)^2. The check computes it for toy feature sets.

import math, random
random.seed(0)
def stats(xs):
    m = sum(xs) / len(xs)
    return m, math.sqrt(sum((x - m) ** 2 for x in xs) / len(xs))
def fid_1d(a, b):
    (ma, sa), (mb, sb) = stats(a), stats(b)
    return (ma - mb) ** 2 + (sa - sb) ** 2
real = [random.gauss(0, 1) for _ in range(5000)]
good = [random.gauss(0.05, 1.0) for _ in range(5000)]       # close to real
shifted = [random.gauss(1.0, 1.0) for _ in range(5000)]     # wrong mean
collapsed = [random.gauss(0, 0.2) for _ in range(5000)]     # mode collapse: too little spread
print("good      FID %.3f" % fid_1d(real, good))
print("shifted   FID %.3f" % fid_1d(real, shifted))
print("collapsed FID %.3f" % fid_1d(real, collapsed))

Output

good      FID 0.002
shifted   FID 1.022
collapsed FID 0.647

FID penalises both a shifted mean (wrong content) and a shrunken spread (lost diversity). Cautions: it needs thousands of samples (10,000 to 50,000), depends on the feature network (Inception was trained on ImageNet), is blind to prompt alignment, is biased for small sample sizes, and can be gamed. Values are only comparable with identical settings. Variants: KID (unbiased), FD-DINOv2 (stronger features), CMMD.

Human evaluation

The final judge. Show raters pairs of images for the same prompt (side-by-side, randomised order) and ask which is better for quality and for prompt match. Use many prompts across categories, several raters per item, and report win rates with confidence intervals (a sign test or bootstrap, as in the AI/ML guide). Use Elo or Bradley-Terry scores to rank several models; arenas collect live votes.

import math
def wilson(wins, n, z=1.96):
    p = wins / n
    d = 1 + z * z / n
    centre = (p + z * z / (2 * n)) / d
    half = z * math.sqrt(p * (1 - p) / n + z * z / (4 * n * n)) / d
    return round(centre - half, 3), round(centre + half, 3)
print("model A preferred in 62 of 100 comparisons ->", wilson(62, 100))
print("model A preferred in 620 of 1000 comparisons ->", wilson(620, 1000))

Output

model A preferred in 62 of 100 comparisons -> (0.522, 0.709)
model A preferred in 620 of 1000 comparisons -> (0.59, 0.65)

Sixty-two of 100 wins is not convincing (the interval contains 0.5); 620 of 1000 is. Sample size matters as much as the win rate.

How this gets asked

FID compares:

  • Single images pixel by pixel No.
  • Gaussian fits to Inception features of real and generated image sets Correct.
  • Prompts only No.
  • Training losses No.

It is a Fréchet distance between two feature distributions.

A model collapses to a few similar images. Which is most likely to reveal it?

  • Very low precision Fidelity can be high.
  • Low recall (coverage) and a worse FID Correct.
  • High CLIPScore only Does not measure diversity.
  • Fewer parameters No.

Mode collapse shrinks the spread of the generated distribution.

Model A wins 62 of 100 human comparisons. What can you say?

  • A is clearly better The interval includes 50 percent.
  • The evidence is weak; more comparisons are needed Correct.
  • B is better No.
  • The raters were wrong No.

A 95 percent interval around 0.62 with n = 100 spans roughly 0.52 to 0.71 and is borderline; always report intervals.

Self-check

What does FID capture and miss?

It captures fidelity and diversity of a distribution in feature space; it misses prompt alignment and is sensitive to sample size and the feature extractor.

Why add human evaluation?

Automatic metrics are proxies; humans judge quality and prompt match directly, with intervals reported.

Training data

What goes in

Foundation image models train on billions of image-text pairs scraped from the web (for example LAION-5B, a list of links and captions), plus licensed and synthetic data. Quality of data affects the model more than most architecture choices.

The data pipeline

  1. Collect image-URL and alt-text pairs; download.
  2. Deduplicate exact and near-duplicate images (perceptual hashing, embeddings): duplicates cause memorisation.
  3. Filter for quality: resolution, aspect ratio, aesthetic score, CLIP image-text similarity (drop mismatched captions), watermark detection, language.
  4. Filter for safety: NSFW classifiers, violence, child safety hash matching, removal requests, personal data.
  5. Re-caption: replace noisy alt-text with detailed synthetic captions from a vision-language model; this strongly improves prompt following (used by DALL-E 3 and SD3).
  6. Bucket by resolution and aspect ratio so batches have uniform shape without cropping everything square.

Memorisation, privacy and copyright

Memorisation

Models can reproduce training images, especially duplicated ones, with few images per concept, or with rare prompts. Studies extracted near-copies of training images from Stable Diffusion by generating many samples for prompts matching duplicated captions and checking for near-duplicates. Mitigations: deduplication, training on more diverse data, limiting overfitting (early stopping, regularisation), differential privacy (costly) and output-side checks.

Copyright and licensing

Legal questions are unsettled and differ by country: whether training on copyrighted images is permitted (fair use or text-and-data-mining exceptions), who owns outputs, and liability for outputs that resemble protected work. Practical approaches: license data (Adobe Firefly, Getty), honour opt-out signals (robots.txt, Do Not Train registries), provide attribution and compensation schemes, filter known characters and logos, and keep documentation of data sources ("datasheets", model cards). State clearly that this is guidance on practice, not legal advice.

Personal likeness and deepfakes

Fine-tuning on a person's photos can produce convincing images of them without consent. Risks: harassment, fraud, non-consensual imagery, misinformation. Responses: consent requirements for likeness training, detection tools, provenance, platform policies and law.

How this gets asked

Which most increases the chance of memorising a training image?

  • Deduplication Reduces it.
  • Many duplicates of the same image with the same caption Correct.
  • More diverse data Reduces it.
  • Lower learning rate Not the main factor.

Repetition during training makes reproduction easy.

Self-check

How to reduce memorisation?

Deduplicate, diversify data, limit overfitting, consider differential privacy, check outputs.

Safety and responsible release

Layers of defence

LayerExamples
DataRemove unsafe content before training
ModelConcept erasure, safety fine-tuning, refusal of harmful prompts
Input filtersPrompt classifiers, blocklists, prompt rewriting
Output filtersImage safety classifiers (the Stable Diffusion safety checker), face and minors detectors
ProvenanceInvisible watermarks and signed metadata
AccessRate limits, accounts, API terms, abuse monitoring
GovernanceRed teaming, model cards, staged release, incident response

No layer is perfect; open weights can have filters removed, so open-model safety relies on data and training choices and on downstream platforms.

Provenance and watermarking

  • Invisible watermarks embedded in pixels or in the generation process (SynthID, Tree-Ring watermarks) survive some edits, but can be removed by aggressive transformations.
  • C2PA content credentials attach signed metadata describing how an image was made; they are lost if metadata is stripped.
  • Detection classifiers are unreliable and arms-race prone.

Combine methods and be honest about their limits.

Bias and representation

Generators reflect the data: prompts such as "a doctor" or "a CEO" skew by gender and race, and many cultures are underrepresented. Measure with audits across prompt sets and demographic attributes, diversify and rebalance data, add prompt-level interventions with care (they can backfire), and publish limitations. Evaluation should include fairness and harm categories, not only image quality.

Red teaming

Test the system adversarially: jailbreak prompts, misspellings, indirect descriptions, languages, image-to-image and inpainting routes around text filters, and combinations of benign concepts. Record failures, fix, and retest regularly.

How this gets asked

Which are layers of safety for image generators? Select all.

  • Filtering training data
  • Prompt and output classifiers
  • Invisible watermarks and provenance metadata
  • Ignoring misuse

Defence in depth is needed because no single layer is reliable.

Self-check

How does watermarking help and where does it fail?

It marks AI content for provenance; it can be removed by edits and does not stop misuse by itself.

Reporting results honestly

  1. State the model, checkpoint, sampler, steps, guidance, resolution and seed policy.
  2. Report metrics on a fixed, public prompt set with enough samples, plus confidence intervals.
  3. Show uncurated samples alongside favourites; say how many images were tried.
  4. Compare at equal compute (network evaluations), not equal steps.
  5. Include failure cases: text rendering, hands, counting, spatial relations.
  6. Document data sources, filtering and known biases in a model card.
Key idea

A good evaluation combines a distribution metric (FID or a stronger variant), a prompt-alignment metric, compositional tests and human preference, with intervals, on a fixed prompt set.

How this gets asked

A sampler uses 25 steps with classifier-free guidance. How many network evaluations per image?

25 steps x 2 passes = 50.

Interview questions: Diffusion evaluation

12 questions. Try to answer aloud in under a minute, then reveal. Tags show the level.

Basic What does FID measure?

The Fréchet distance between Gaussian fits of Inception features of real and generated images: lower means the generated distribution is closer to the real one.

Basic What does CLIPScore measure?

How well an image matches its text prompt, as the cosine similarity of CLIP embeddings.

Medium What are the limits of FID?

It needs many samples, depends on the ImageNet-trained feature network, ignores prompt alignment, is biased for small sample sizes and can be gamed. Use KID, FD-DINOv2 and human evaluation alongside it.

Medium How do you run a human evaluation?

Show randomised side-by-side pairs for the same prompt, ask about quality and prompt match, use many prompts and raters, and report win rates with confidence intervals or Elo scores.

Medium Why re-caption training images?

Web alt-text is short and noisy; detailed synthetic captions improve prompt following and compositional accuracy.

Medium What does a data pipeline for image generation involve?

Collection, deduplication, quality and aesthetic filtering, safety filtering, re-captioning and bucketing by resolution.

Advanced How can a diffusion model memorise training images and how do you reduce it?

Duplicated images and captions make near-copies likely. Deduplicate, diversify data, limit overfitting, apply output checks and consider differential privacy.

Advanced What layers of safety do image generators use?

Data filtering, model-level safety training, prompt classifiers, output classifiers, provenance watermarking, access controls and red teaming. No single layer is sufficient.

Advanced How does watermarking help and what are its limits?

Invisible watermarks and C2PA credentials signal AI origin, but they can be stripped by edits or metadata removal, so they supplement rather than replace other controls.

Advanced How would you test for bias?

Audit prompt sets across occupations, attributes and cultures, measure demographic distributions of outputs, compare with references, and iterate on data and training rather than only prompt rewriting.

Advanced What belongs in a good evaluation report?

Model, sampler, steps, guidance, seeds, a fixed public prompt set, enough samples, uncurated samples, metrics with intervals, compute-matched comparisons and failure cases.

Advanced Why compare samplers or models at equal network evaluations?

Steps are not equal cost: Heun doubles evaluations and guidance doubles them again, so equal steps can hide large compute differences.
