Part DX10

Data curation and troubleshooting: building image datasets and fixing common diffusion failures

Most diffusion projects succeed or fail on the data and on the ability to diagnose what is wrong with samples. This part treats both as engineering: a repeatable data pipeline with measurable filters, and a table that maps each visible symptom to its likely causes and the experiment that tells them apart.

MediumAdvanced~4 hours

Data is the model

For a fixed architecture, quality, style, diversity and safety are largely determined by the dataset. Recent progress in text-to-image models owes as much to better captions, filtering and curation as to new architectures. Treat the dataset like code: versioned, tested and documented.

Collection and licensing

  • Sources: licensed or public-domain collections, your own photos or renders, commissioned data, web crawls (large, noisy, legally contested), synthetic data.
  • Legal and ethical checks: licence terms and permitted uses, copyright and opt-out signals (robots.txt, noai flags, registries), personal data and biometric rules, consent for people's likenesses, and removal of illegal content. Keep provenance records (source, licence, date) per item so that you can remove content on request.
  • Small custom sets (style or subject fine-tuning): 10 to 30 clean, varied images are often enough for LoRA or DreamBooth (DX4); quality and variety beat count.

Self-check

What provenance should each training item carry?

Source, licence, date and any opt-out status, so content can be audited and removed.

Filtering

Common filters, each a threshold you can measure and tune:

FilterRemovesHow
Resolution and aspect ratioTiny images, extreme cropsMetadata checks; set minimum short side

Watermark and text overlaysStock-photo watermarks, logosClassifiers; they otherwise appear in generations

A filter is a trade-off: stricter thresholds raise average quality but shrink the data and may remove diversity (certain styles, skin tones or regions are scored lower by biased scorers). Always look at what was removed.

import random
random.seed(1)
# a synthetic pool of 10,000 images with a quality score and a CLIP-style text alignment score
pool = [{"quality": random.gauss(5.2, 1.2), "clip": random.gauss(0.27, 0.05), "group": random.choice(["photo", "art", "diagram"])} for _ in range(10000)]
# the "art" group is systematically scored lower by the quality model (a bias to detect)
for item in pool:
    if item["group"] == "art": item["quality"] -= 0.6
def keep(item, q=5.0, c=0.25): return item["quality"] >= q and item["clip"] >= c
kept = [i for i in pool if keep(i)]
print(f"kept {len(kept):,} of {len(pool):,} images ({len(kept) / len(pool):.0%}) with quality >= 5.0 and CLIP >= 0.25")
for g in ("photo", "art", "diagram"):
    before = sum(i["group"] == g for i in pool) / len(pool); after = sum(i["group"] == g for i in kept) / len(kept)
    print(f"  {g:>8}: {before:.0%} of the pool -> {after:.0%} of the filtered set")

Output

kept 3,275 of 10,000 images (33%) with quality >= 5.0 and CLIP >= 0.25
     photo: 33% of the pool -> 39% of the filtered set
       art: 33% of the pool -> 25% of the filtered set
   diagram: 34% of the pool -> 36% of the filtered set

The strict filter changed the mix: the biased scorer under-rates "art", so its share falls. Checking group proportions before and after is a cheap way to catch such side effects; remedies include per-group thresholds, a better scorer or sampling to restore balance.

How this gets asked

Why compare group proportions before and after a quality filter?

  • To save disk No.
  • A biased scorer can remove whole styles or groups, reducing diversity without being obvious in averages Correct.
  • To speed training No.
  • Because filters are random No.

Inspect what was removed, not only what remains.

De-duplication

Duplicates cause memorisation (the model reproduces specific training images, a privacy and copyright risk) and skew the distribution toward whatever is repeated. Use exact hashes (file or pixel hashes) for identical files and near-duplicate detection for resized, cropped or recompressed copies: perceptual hashes (aHash, dHash, pHash) or embedding similarity (CLIP or specialised copy-detection models with an approximate nearest-neighbour index, A4).

The difference hash (dHash) compares each pixel with its right neighbour on a tiny grayscale thumbnail, giving a bit string that survives resizing, brightness changes and light compression; images are near-duplicates when the Hamming distance is small.

import random
random.seed(2)
def make_image(seed, size=16):
    r = random.Random(seed); return [[r.randint(0, 255) for _ in range(size)] for _ in range(size)]
def downscale(img, size=9):                                    # nearest-neighbour thumbnail to size x size
    n = len(img); return [[img[y * n // size][x * n // size] for x in range(size)] for y in range(size)]
def dhash(img):
    t = downscale(img, 9)
    return [1 if t[y][x] > t[y][x + 1] else 0 for y in range(8) for x in range(8)]       # 64 bits: is the pixel brighter than its right neighbour?
def hamming(a, b): return sum(x != y for x, y in zip(a, b))
original = make_image(1)
brighter = [[min(255, p + 20) for p in row] for row in original]                          # brightness change
noisy = [[max(0, min(255, p + random.randint(-6, 6))) for p in row] for row in original]  # mild recompression-like noise
different = make_image(2)
print("Hamming distance (0 to 64) between 64-bit dHashes:")
print("  original vs brightened:", hamming(dhash(original), dhash(brighter)))
print("  original vs lightly noisy:", hamming(dhash(original), dhash(noisy)))
print("  original vs a different image:", hamming(dhash(original), dhash(different)), "(about 32 for unrelated images)")

Output

Hamming distance (0 to 64) between 64-bit dHashes:
  original vs brightened: 0
  original vs lightly noisy: 1
  original vs a different image: 34 (about 32 for unrelated images)

Practical pipeline: compute hashes or embeddings for the whole set, find neighbours below a threshold (use locality-sensitive hashing or an ANN index so it is not O(n^2)), keep the best representative of each cluster (highest resolution or quality), and also remove training images that are near-duplicates of evaluation or benchmark images to avoid leakage.

How this gets asked

Why de-duplicate training images?

  • To make files smaller only No.
  • Duplicates cause memorisation of specific images and skew the distribution Correct.
  • To add noise No.
  • To enable guidance No.

Also remove near-duplicates of evaluation images.

What does a small Hamming distance between two perceptual hashes mean?

  • The images are unrelated No.
  • The images are visually similar (likely near-duplicates) Correct.
  • The files are the same size No.
  • The images have different colours No.

dHash survives resizing and light recompression.

Self-check

How do you find near-duplicates at scale?

Perceptual hashes with locality-sensitive hashing or embeddings with an approximate nearest-neighbour index.

Captions

Text-to-image models are only as good as their captions. Raw alt text is short, noisy and often unrelated ("IMG_2031", "buy now"). Modern practice:

  • Re-caption with a strong vision-language model to produce detailed, accurate descriptions (objects, attributes, relations, style, text in the image, composition).
  • Mix synthetic captions with a fraction of original captions (for example 80 to 95% synthetic) so the model still handles terse, human-style prompts.
  • Keep captions consistent in format, avoid hallucinated details (verify against the image or use detection tools), and include negative information only if you intend to train with it.
  • Use caption dropout (an empty caption about 10% of the time) so classifier-free guidance works (DX2).
  • For fine-tuning on a subject, use a rare token or identifier plus a class word ("a photo of sks dog") and caption everything else you do not want baked in (otherwise the model ties it to the subject).
import re
captions = ["IMG_2031.jpg", "buy now cheap!!!", "A red bicycle leaning against a brick wall in soft morning light, shallow depth of field", "dog", "Photo of a golden retriever puppy playing with a blue ball on green grass"]
def caption_quality(c):
    words = re.findall(r"[A-Za-z]+", c); issues = []
    if len(words) < 4: issues.append("too short")
    if re.search(r"\.(jpg|png)$|img_\d+", c, re.I): issues.append("filename")
    if re.search(r"buy|cheap|click|sale", c, re.I): issues.append("spam words")
    return issues
for c in captions: print(f"{c[:56]:<58}-> {'OK' if not caption_quality(c) else caption_quality(c)}")

A red bicycle leaning against a brick wall in soft morni  -> OK
dog                                                       -> ['too short']
Photo of a golden retriever puppy playing with a blue ba  -> OK

Why use caption dropout during training?

  • To save labels No.
  • So the model also learns an unconditional prediction, which classifier-free guidance needs Correct.
  • To shorten captions No.
  • To remove noise No.

Typically about 10% of captions are replaced by an empty string.

Mostly detailed synthetic captions with a fraction of original ones, plus caption dropout for guidance.

Balancing and sampling

Web data follows a long tail: a few concepts (selfies, cats, sunsets) dominate and rare ones are almost absent. The model then draws the common ones well and the rare ones poorly. Counter with concept-balanced sampling (cluster embeddings and sample clusters more evenly), up-weighting rare high-quality concepts, and checking coverage with a held-out concept list. Also balance resolutions and aspect ratios with bucketing: group images of similar shapes into batches so no image is distorted by cropping to a square.

import random
random.seed(3)
concepts = {"cat": 5000, "sunset": 4000, "car": 2500, "lighthouse": 120, "abacus": 30}
total = sum(concepts.values())
print("natural share:", {k: f"{v / total:.1%}" for k, v in concepts.items()})
def balanced_weights(counts, temperature=0.5):                         # sample proportional to count ** temperature (1 = natural, 0 = uniform)
    w = {k: v ** temperature for k, v in counts.items()}; z = sum(w.values()); return {k: v / z for k, v in w.items()}
for temp in (1.0, 0.5, 0.0):
    print(f"temperature {temp}:", {k: f"{v:.1%}" for k, v in balanced_weights(concepts, temp).items()})
print("an image of an 'abacus' appears in about", f"{balanced_weights(concepts, 0.5)['abacus'] / (30 / total):.0f}x", "more batches than its natural frequency would give (so repeat epochs on rare images matter: watch for memorisation)")

How this gets asked

How does aspect-ratio bucketing help?

  • It adds more data No.
  • Images of similar shapes are batched together so none are distorted by square cropping, and the model learns varied aspect ratios Correct.
  • It speeds the VAE No.
  • It removes duplicates No.

Each batch comes from a single bucket so tensors match in shape.

Resolution buckets

def bucket(width, height, buckets=((1024, 1024), (1152, 896), (896, 1152), (1344, 768), (768, 1344))):
    ar = width / height
    return min(buckets, key=lambda b: abs(b[0] / b[1] - ar))
for size in [(4000, 3000), (1080, 1920), (1000, 1000), (3000, 1300)]:
    print(f"{size} (aspect {size[0] / size[1]:.2f}) -> trained in bucket {bucket(*size)}")

Output

(4000, 3000) (aspect 1.33) -> trained in bucket (1152, 896)
(1080, 1920) (aspect 0.56) -> trained in bucket (768, 1344)
(1000, 1000) (aspect 1.00) -> trained in bucket (1024, 1024)
(3000, 1300) (aspect 2.31) -> trained in bucket (1344, 768)

Batches are drawn from one bucket at a time so tensors have equal shapes; the model learns to generate each aspect ratio and the conditioning includes the target size.

Troubleshooting guide

Diagnose by symptom, then run the cheapest experiment that separates the likely causes.

SymptomLikely causesWhat to check or try
Pure noise or colour blobs after trainingBug in the loss target, schedule or sampler; wrong normalisationOverfit 10 images first; verify forward noising reconstructs; check data range is [-1, 1]; sample with the training schedule
Blurry, low-detail samplesToo few training steps; low capacity; MSE averaging at high noise; VAE too lossyTrain longer; check VAE reconstructions; use more sampling steps; try EMA weights
Washed-out grey or mid-tone images; cannot make very dark or bright imagesNon-zero terminal SNR (the schedule never reaches pure noise) so the model leaks mean brightnessZero-terminal-SNR rescaling, noise offset, v-prediction (DX1)
Oversaturated, burnt coloursGuidance scale too high; no rescalingLower the scale (5 to 8), use CFG rescale or dynamic thresholding

Distorted anatomy (hands, faces)Insufficient examples; resolution too low; weak conditioningMore and better data; higher resolution; inpaint fixes; larger model

A debugging order that saves time: (1) overfit a tiny dataset to near-perfect samples, (2) check the schedule and data ranges, (3) verify sampling code with a model you trust, (4) inspect samples at several checkpoints and noise levels, (5) scale up only after small runs behave.

# loss per timestep bucket tells you where the model is weak: early diffusion times are easy, high-noise times are hard
import random
random.seed(4)
buckets = ["t 0-100", "t 100-300", "t 300-600", "t 600-1000"]
base = [0.002, 0.010, 0.040, 0.120]                             # a typical shape of per-timestep MSE: small at low noise, larger at high noise
logged = [[b * random.uniform(0.8, 1.2) for _ in range(50)] for b in base]
for name, vals in zip(buckets, logged): print(f"{name:>11}: mean loss {sum(vals) / len(vals):.4f}")
print("a single averaged training loss would hide that the high-noise buckets, where global structure forms, are the ones that matter; log losses per bucket")

Output

    t 0-100: mean loss 0.0020
  t 100-300: mean loss 0.0099
  t 300-600: mean loss 0.0394
 t 600-1000: mean loss 0.1232
a single averaged training loss would hide that the high-noise buckets, where global structure forms, are the ones that matter; log losses per bucket

How this gets asked

Samples are washed-out grey and the model cannot produce very dark images. Most likely cause?

  • Learning rate too high No.
  • The noise schedule does not reach zero signal-to-noise at the end, leaking the mean brightness; use zero-terminal-SNR or v-prediction Correct.
  • Too many steps No.
  • A broken GPU No.

Fix the schedule, then retrain or fine-tune.

What is the first debugging experiment for a new diffusion implementation?

  • Train on a billion images Slow.
  • Overfit a tiny dataset (about 10 images) until samples reproduce them Correct.
  • Tune guidance Later.
  • Swap the optimiser Not first.

If it cannot overfit ten images, there is a bug.

Training loss is low but samples are poor. Why might that happen?

  • The loss is wrong always No.
  • The averaged loss is dominated by easy timesteps; check loss per noise level and evaluate samples directly Correct.
  • The dataset is too small Not necessarily.
  • The GPU is too slow No.

Log samples at fixed seeds and per-bucket losses.

Self-check

Fixes for oversaturated images?

Lower guidance scale, CFG rescale or dynamic thresholding.

A checklist before a big run

  • Data: counts per stage, de-duplicated, filtered with inspected examples, captions sampled and read, licences recorded, evaluation images held out and decontaminated.
  • Pipeline: overfit-10-images test passes; forward and reverse processes verified numerically; sampler reproduces training images from noise at the end of the overfit test.
  • Training: mixed precision, gradient clipping, EMA, learning-rate warm-up, checkpointing with resume tested, deterministic data order by step, logging of per-timestep loss and sample grids at fixed seeds.
  • Evaluation: fixed prompt set covering rare concepts, metrics (FID, CLIP score, human comparison), safety checks and memorisation checks (DX5).
  • Budget: estimated compute and cost, early-stopping criteria, and a plan for what you will change if the first run underperforms.
Key idea

Curate the data like code: license, filter with measured thresholds, de-duplicate with perceptual hashes or embeddings, re-caption carefully, balance concepts and aspect ratios, and check what each step removed. Debug by symptom: first overfit a tiny set, then check ranges, schedules and samplers, and log per-timestep losses and fixed-seed sample grids.

Self-check

Name two checks before a large training run.

The overfit-ten-images test and a verified resume-from-checkpoint (plus fixed-seed sample logging).

Interview questions: Data and troubleshooting

14 questions. Try to answer aloud in under a minute, then reveal. Tags show the level.

Basic Why de-duplicate training data?

Duplicates cause memorisation and bias the distribution toward repeated content; also decontaminate against evaluation images.

Basic What is the first test of a new diffusion implementation?

Overfit about ten images until samples reproduce them; failure indicates a bug in the loss, schedule, data range or sampler.

Medium How do you find near-duplicate images?

Perceptual hashes such as dHash with Hamming distance, or embedding similarity with an approximate nearest-neighbour index.

Medium Why re-caption a dataset?

Alt text is short and noisy; detailed accurate captions greatly improve prompt following, usually mixed with some original captions.

Medium What is caption dropout and why use it?

Replacing captions with an empty string about 10% of the time so the model learns an unconditional prediction for classifier-free guidance.

Medium What causes washed-out grey samples and how do you fix them?

A schedule whose final signal-to-noise ratio is not zero leaks mean brightness; use zero-terminal-SNR rescaling, v-prediction or a noise offset.

Medium Samples are oversaturated. What do you change?

Lower the guidance scale, use CFG rescaling or dynamic thresholding.

Medium How do you detect memorisation?

Find nearest training images for generated samples in embedding space and check duplication rates, then reduce duplicates, epochs or model capacity.

Medium What is aspect-ratio bucketing?

Grouping images of similar shapes into batches so there is no square cropping, and the model learns varied aspect ratios.

Advanced How can a quality filter harm diversity?

A biased scorer rates some styles or groups lower, so strict thresholds shift the mix; compare group proportions before and after and use per-group thresholds.

Advanced Training loss is low but samples look bad. What do you check?

Per-timestep loss (high-noise levels matter), sampler correctness, EMA weights, and direct sample inspection at fixed seeds rather than the averaged loss.

Advanced How do you fine-tune on a subject without forgetting the base model?

Use LoRA with a low learning rate and few steps, a rare identifier token and prior-preservation class images, and caption everything that should vary.

Advanced What licensing and consent checks belong in a data pipeline?

Licence terms and permitted use, opt-out signals, personal data and likeness consent, illegal content removal and provenance records enabling deletion.

Advanced How do you balance a long-tailed concept distribution?

Sample with count raised to a temperature below one, cluster embeddings to balance concepts, up-weight rare high-quality items, and watch for memorisation from repetition.
