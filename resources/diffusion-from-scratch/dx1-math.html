The mathematics of diffusion: DDPM derived step by step

The main guide gave you the working recipe. This part derives why the recipe is right. Every identity is checked by a short program, so you can trust the formulas and rebuild them in an interview.

Advanced~5 hours

The forward process as a Gaussian chain

Definition

Pick a noise schedule beta_1 ... beta_T with each value between 0 and 1. The forward process is a Markov chain: each step adds a little Gaussian noise and shrinks the signal slightly.

One forward step
q(x_t | x_(t-1)) = Normal( sqrt(1 - beta_t) x_(t-1), beta_t I )
beta_t
variance of the noise added at step t
sqrt(1 - beta_t)
how much of the previous image is kept
I
identity, so every pixel gets independent noise

Equivalent sampling form (the reparameterisation trick): x_t = sqrt(alpha_t) x_(t-1) + sqrt(beta_t) z, with alpha_t = 1 - beta_t and z standard normal. The scaling by sqrt(alpha_t) keeps the variance from exploding: if x_(t-1) has variance 1, then x_t has variance alpha_t + beta_t = 1.

The closed form by induction

Write alpha_bar_t = alpha_1 alpha_2 ... alpha_t. Claim: x_t = sqrt(alpha_bar_t) x_0 + sqrt(1 - alpha_bar_t) eps for one standard normal eps.

  1. For t = 1 it is the definition.
  2. Suppose it holds at t - 1. Then x_t = sqrt(alpha_t) (sqrt(alpha_bar_(t-1)) x_0 + sqrt(1 - alpha_bar_(t-1)) e1) + sqrt(beta_t) e2.
  3. The two noise terms are independent Gaussians, so they merge into one Gaussian whose variance is the sum of variances: alpha_t (1 - alpha_bar_(t-1)) + beta_t = alpha_t - alpha_bar_t + 1 - alpha_t = 1 - alpha_bar_t.
  4. The signal coefficient multiplies: sqrt(alpha_t) sqrt(alpha_bar_(t-1)) = sqrt(alpha_bar_t). That completes the induction.

The check below simulates the chain step by step and compares with the closed form.

import random, math
random.seed(0)
T = 50
betas = [1e-4 + (0.05 - 1e-4) * i / (T - 1) for i in range(T)]
alpha_bar = []
p = 1.0
for b in betas:
    p *= 1 - b
    alpha_bar.append(p)

x0, n = 1.0, 20000
final = []
for _ in range(n):
    x = x0
    for b in betas:                                   # step by step through the chain
        x = math.sqrt(1 - b) * x + math.sqrt(b) * random.gauss(0, 1)
    final.append(x)
mean = sum(final) / n
var = sum((v - mean) ** 2 for v in final) / n
print("simulated  mean %.3f  var %.3f" % (mean, var))
print("closed form mean %.3f  var %.3f" % (math.sqrt(alpha_bar[-1]) * x0, 1 - alpha_bar[-1]))

Output

simulated  mean 0.533  var 0.719
closed form mean 0.529  var 0.720

What the schedule must achieve

  • alpha_bar_T should be close to 0, so x_T is almost pure noise and the sampler can start from Normal(0, I).
  • Early steps should destroy little information, so each reverse step is a small, easy correction (a reverse step of a small Gaussian step is itself approximately Gaussian, which is what makes the model's job tractable).

How this gets asked

Why does the forward step use sqrt(1 - beta_t) x_(t-1) instead of just x_(t-1)?

  • To make the image brighter No.
  • To keep the variance from growing: shrinking the signal while adding noise keeps total variance near 1 Correct.
  • To avoid needing a schedule No.
  • Because beta_t is negative No.

The variance of x_t is alpha_t + beta_t = 1 when x_(t-1) has variance 1.

alpha_1 = 0.9, alpha_2 = 0.8, alpha_3 = 0.5. What is alpha_bar_3?

0.9 x 0.8 x 0.5 = 0.36.

Self-check

Derive the closed form.

Merge independent Gaussians by adding variances, multiply the signal coefficients, and use induction to get sqrt(alpha_bar) x_0 + sqrt(1 - alpha_bar) eps.

The reverse process and its posterior

The target of learning

We want to sample by running the chain backwards: p(x_(t-1) | x_t). That distribution depends on the unknown data distribution, so we learn it. But if we also knew x_0, the reverse step would be exactly computable.

The posterior q(x_(t-1) | x_t, x_0)

By Bayes, it is proportional to q(x_t | x_(t-1)) q(x_(t-1) | x_0), a product of two Gaussians, which is Gaussian:

Posterior mean and variance
mu_tilde(x_t, x_0) = [ sqrt(alpha_bar_(t-1)) beta_t / (1 - alpha_bar_t) ] x_0 + [ sqrt(alpha_t) (1 - alpha_bar_(t-1)) / (1 - alpha_bar_t) ] x_t ; beta_tilde_t = (1 - alpha_bar_(t-1)) / (1 - alpha_bar_t) beta_t
mu_tilde
the exact mean of the reverse step given the clean image
beta_tilde_t
its variance, a little smaller than beta_t

The mean is a weighted average of the clean image and the current noisy image. The check multiplies the two Gaussians directly (adding precisions) and compares with the formula.

import math
T = 1000
betas = [1e-4 + (0.02 - 1e-4) * i / (T - 1) for i in range(T)]
alphas = [1 - b for b in betas]
ab = []
p = 1.0
for a in alphas:
    p *= a
    ab.append(p)

t = 400                      # index of x_t; x_(t-1) uses ab[t-1]
x0, xt = 0.8, 0.3
a_t, b_t, ab_t, ab_prev = alphas[t], betas[t], ab[t], ab[t - 1]

# direct Bayes: prior x_(t-1) | x0 ~ N(sqrt(ab_prev) x0, 1 - ab_prev); likelihood xt | x_(t-1) ~ N(sqrt(a_t) x_(t-1), b_t)
prior_mean, prior_var = math.sqrt(ab_prev) * x0, 1 - ab_prev
precision = 1 / prior_var + a_t / b_t
post_var = 1 / precision
post_mean = post_var * (prior_mean / prior_var + math.sqrt(a_t) * xt / b_t)

# closed form from the formula
mu = (math.sqrt(ab_prev) * b_t / (1 - ab_t)) * x0 + (math.sqrt(a_t) * (1 - ab_prev) / (1 - ab_t)) * xt
var = (1 - ab_prev) / (1 - ab_t) * b_t
print(round(post_mean - mu, 12), round(post_var - var, 12))
print("posterior std is %.5f vs beta std %.5f" % (math.sqrt(var), math.sqrt(b_t)))

Output

0.0 0.0
posterior std is 0.08973 vs beta std 0.08982

From the clean image to a noise prediction

We do not know x_0 at sampling time, but x_t = sqrt(alpha_bar_t) x_0 + sqrt(1 - alpha_bar_t) eps lets us eliminate it: x_0 = (x_t - sqrt(1 - alpha_bar_t) eps) / sqrt(alpha_bar_t). Substituting into mu_tilde and simplifying gives:

Mean in terms of predicted noise
mu_theta(x_t, t) = (1 / sqrt(alpha_t)) ( x_t - [ beta_t / sqrt(1 - alpha_bar_t) ] eps_theta(x_t, t) )
eps_theta
the network's noise prediction

This is exactly the sampling line in sample.py of Phase 3, with beta_t / sqrt(1 - alpha_bar_t) equal to (1 - alpha_t) / sqrt(1 - alpha_bar_t). So the network never needs to output an image: predicting the noise is the same as predicting the clean image, one linear equation away.

Key idea

Three equivalent outputs: predict the noise eps, the clean image x_0, or the posterior mean mu. Each is a fixed linear combination of the other two and x_t, so choosing one is a modelling and loss-scaling decision, not a change of what is learned.

How this gets asked

  • Bernoulli No.
  • Unknown It is known in closed form.

Its mean is a weighted average of x_0 and x_t.

Which pair is exactly interchangeable given x_t and alpha_bar_t?

  • The learning rate and eps No.
  • Predicting eps and predicting x_0 Correct.
  • Batch size and v No.

x_0 = (x_t - sqrt(1 - alpha_bar) eps) / sqrt(alpha_bar).

Self-check

What is the posterior mean?

A weighted average of x_0 and x_t, with weights from alpha_bar_(t-1), alpha_t and beta_t; substituting x_0 in terms of eps gives the sampling formula.

The training objective from the variational bound

The ELBO in one paragraph

Treat x_1 ... x_T as latent variables. Maximising the data likelihood is intractable, so we maximise the evidence lower bound (ELBO). Because both the forward chain and the model's reverse steps are Gaussian, the bound breaks into a sum of KL divergences between q(x_(t-1) | x_t, x_0) and p_theta(x_(t-1) | x_t). The KL between two Gaussians with the same variance is a squared difference of means scaled by the variance.

Per-step term of the bound
L_t = E [ ( beta_t^2 / ( 2 sigma_t^2 alpha_t (1 - alpha_bar_t) ) ) norm(eps - eps_theta(x_t, t))^2 ]
sigma_t^2
variance chosen for the reverse step

Why the simple loss works better

Ho et al. dropped the weight and trained on the unweighted loss, L_simple = E [ norm(eps - eps_theta(x_t, t))^2 ] with t uniform. The unweighted version down-weights the easy low-noise steps relative to the true bound and emphasises harder, noisier denoising. The result was better sample quality, though a worse likelihood score. Improved DDPM later learned the variance to recover likelihood.

import math
T = 1000
betas = [1e-4 + (0.02 - 1e-4) * i / (T - 1) for i in range(T)]
alphas = [1 - b for b in betas]
ab, p = [], 1.0
for a in alphas:
    p *= a
    ab.append(p)
# weight of the true ELBO term with sigma_t^2 = beta_t, relative to the simple loss (weight 1)
for t in (1, 10, 100, 500, 999):
    w = betas[t] ** 2 / (2 * betas[t] * alphas[t] * (1 - ab[t]))
    print("t=%3d  ELBO weight %.4f" % (t, w))

Output

t=  1  ELBO weight 0.2727
t= 10  ELBO weight 0.0682
t=100  ELBO weight 0.0100
t=500  ELBO weight 0.0055
t=999  ELBO weight 0.0102

The true bound puts far more weight on the first, least noisy steps (0.27 at t = 1 against about 0.01 from t = 100 on), which the simple loss treats as equal to every other step. That is the reweighting the text describes.

Signal-to-noise ratio and parameterisations

SNR

At step t the signal power is alpha_bar_t and the noise power is 1 - alpha_bar_t:

Signal-to-noise ratio
SNR(t) = alpha_bar_t / (1 - alpha_bar_t) ; log-SNR = log SNR(t)
a monotonic quantity
high at t = 0, near zero at t = T

Every schedule is really a path of log-SNR through time. The linear schedule spends many steps at very low SNR, where the image is already gone, which is why the cosine schedule helps: it spreads log-SNR more evenly. High-resolution images keep structure at the same SNR for longer than small ones, so the best schedule shifts with resolution; modern models shift the schedule.

import math
T = 1000
betas = [1e-4 + (0.02 - 1e-4) * i / (T - 1) for i in range(T)]
ab, p = [], 1.0
for b in betas:
    p *= 1 - b
    ab.append(p)
for t in (0, 250, 500, 750, 999):
    snr = ab[t] / (1 - ab[t])
    print("t=%3d  alpha_bar=%.5f  log SNR=%6.2f" % (t, ab[t], math.log(snr)))

The conversions are exact linear maps, verified here:

import math, random
random.seed(1)
ab = 0.3                                   # any value in (0, 1)
x0, eps = random.gauss(0, 1), random.gauss(0, 1)
xt = math.sqrt(ab) * x0 + math.sqrt(1 - ab) * eps
v = math.sqrt(ab) * eps - math.sqrt(1 - ab) * x0

x0_from_eps = (xt - math.sqrt(1 - ab) * eps) / math.sqrt(ab)
x0_from_v = math.sqrt(ab) * xt - math.sqrt(1 - ab) * v
eps_from_v = math.sqrt(1 - ab) * xt + math.sqrt(ab) * v
print(round(x0 - x0_from_eps, 12), round(x0 - x0_from_v, 12), round(eps - eps_from_v, 12))

Output

0.0 0.0 0.0

Loss weighting

Switching target changes the effective weighting over t of the loss on the image. Practical schemes: min-SNR-gamma (cap the weight at high SNR, speeds convergence), P2 weighting, and v-loss. When quality looks off at some noise range, check the weighting before the architecture.

Score matching and the SDE view

The score

The score of a distribution is the gradient of its log density, grad_x log p(x): it points toward higher probability. If you can estimate the score of the noisy data at every noise level, you can generate by following it (plus noise), which is Langevin dynamics.

Tweedie's link to noise prediction

For x_t = sqrt(alpha_bar_t) x_0 + sqrt(1 - alpha_bar_t) eps, the score of the noisy marginal satisfies:

Noise prediction is the score
grad log p_t(x_t) = - eps / sqrt(1 - alpha_bar_t)

Check on the simplest case: data Normal(0, s^2). The noisy marginal is Normal(0, v) with v = alpha_bar s^2 + 1 - alpha_bar, whose score is -x / v, so the optimal noise predictor is linear: eps_opt(x) = sqrt(1 - alpha_bar) x / v. A regression of eps on x_t from samples should recover the same coefficient.

import math, random
random.seed(2)
s, ab = 2.0, 0.4
v = ab * s * s + 1 - ab
analytic = math.sqrt(1 - ab) / v
xs, es = [], []
for _ in range(200000):
    x0, e = random.gauss(0, s), random.gauss(0, 1)
    xs.append(math.sqrt(ab) * x0 + math.sqrt(1 - ab) * e)
    es.append(e)
coef = sum(x * e for x, e in zip(xs, es)) / sum(x * x for x in xs)    # least-squares slope of eps on x_t
print("analytic coefficient %.4f, regression %.4f" % (analytic, coef))

Output

analytic coefficient 0.3521, regression 0.3527

Continuous time: SDEs

Let the number of steps go to infinity. The forward chain becomes a stochastic differential equation. The variance-preserving form (DDPM's limit) is dx = -1/2 beta(t) x dt + sqrt(beta(t)) dw. Anderson's result says the reverse-time SDE is dx = [ -1/2 beta x - beta score ] dt + sqrt(beta) dw_bar: the same dynamics with the score added. There is also a deterministic probability flow ODE with the same marginals, dx/dt = -1/2 beta(t) [ x + score ], which DDIM discretises. Seeing diffusion as a smooth ODE or SDE lets us borrow numerical solvers (DX2).

Interview questions: Diffusion math

13 questions. Try to answer aloud in under a minute, then reveal. Tags show the level.

Basic What does one forward diffusion step do?

It scales the previous image by sqrt(1 - beta_t) and adds Gaussian noise of variance beta_t, so signal shrinks as noise is added while the overall variance stays near 1.

Basic Why can you jump straight to step t?

Composing Gaussian steps gives another Gaussian: x_t = sqrt(alpha_bar_t) x_0 + sqrt(1 - alpha_bar_t) eps, because independent Gaussians add by summing variances.

Basic What is alpha_bar_t?

The cumulative product of alpha_s = 1 - beta_s up to t. It is the fraction of the original signal variance that survives at step t.

Medium Derive the closed form of the forward process by induction.

Assume x_(t-1) = sqrt(ab_(t-1)) x_0 + sqrt(1 - ab_(t-1)) e1. Then x_t = sqrt(alpha_t) x_(t-1) + sqrt(beta_t) e2. The noise terms merge with variance alpha_t (1 - ab_(t-1)) + beta_t = 1 - ab_t, and the signal coefficient is sqrt(alpha_t ab_(t-1)) = sqrt(ab_t).

Medium What is the posterior q(x_(t-1) | x_t, x_0) and why is it useful?

A Gaussian with mean a weighted average of x_0 and x_t and variance beta_tilde_t = (1 - ab_(t-1)) / (1 - ab_t) beta_t. It is the exact target for each reverse step if x_0 were known, so the model learns to approximate its mean.

Medium How does the network's noise prediction give the reverse mean?

Replace x_0 by (x_t - sqrt(1 - ab_t) eps) / sqrt(ab_t) in the posterior mean to get mu = (x_t - beta_t / sqrt(1 - ab_t) eps_theta) / sqrt(alpha_t).

Medium Why is the simple loss better than the true ELBO weighting for samples?

The ELBO weights low-noise steps heavily; dropping the weights emphasises harder, noisier denoising, which improved perceptual quality at the cost of a worse likelihood.

Medium What is SNR in diffusion and why do schedules care?

alpha_bar / (1 - alpha_bar). A schedule is a path of log-SNR over time; a good one spreads steps over the noise range where structure is formed, which is why cosine beats linear.

Advanced Compare eps, x0 and v prediction.

They are linear reparameterisations of each other given x_t. eps is stable at low noise but amplifies errors at very high noise, x0 is blurry at low noise, and v = sqrt(ab) eps - sqrt(1 - ab) x0 is well scaled at all levels and works with zero terminal SNR.

Advanced How is a diffusion model related to score matching?

The noisy marginal's score equals - eps / sqrt(1 - alpha_bar), so training eps_theta by regression is denoising score matching and eps_theta gives the score up to a known scale.

Advanced What is the probability flow ODE?

A deterministic ODE with the same marginals as the reverse SDE, dx/dt = f(x, t) - 1/2 g(t)^2 score. DDIM and most fast samplers discretise it.

Advanced What variance should the reverse step use?

sigma_t^2 = beta_t or beta_tilde_t are the two fixed extremes. Improved DDPM learns an interpolation between them, which improves likelihood and allows fewer steps.

Advanced Why must alpha_bar_T be close to zero?

Sampling starts from Normal(0, I). If the forward process leaves signal at T the start distribution mismatches the training one, causing artefacts such as washed-out images.
